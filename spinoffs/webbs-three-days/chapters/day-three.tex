\chapter{Day Three}

Three in the morning. He'd stopped trying to sleep around two.

The city through the office window. Every light a fixed point in a network he couldn't stop computing. The traffic signal at the corner---red, green, red, green---and the intervals between state changes, and the relationship between those intervals and the flow of vehicles, and the relationship between that flow and the flow of pedestrians, and the relationship between all of these flows and the time of night and the neighborhood's economic topology and the transit schedules and the---

He pressed his palms against his eyes. Counted to ten. Opened them.

The network was still there.

Rachel sleeping. He could hear her breathing through the wall. Twelve cycles per minute. He loved the sound. He'd loved it since the first night she'd stayed over, when he'd lain awake in his apartment listening to her breathe and thinking: \textit{this person trusts me enough to sleep next to me.} Simple thought. Human-scale. The kind of thought that fits in a normal mind without pressing against the edges.

He still had the thought. Still had the feeling underneath it. The love was vivid, present, taking up residence in his chest the way it always had---warm, heavy, real. He could feel it the way you feel sunlight on your skin. Unmistakable.

His hands were shaking. Had been since he woke up. Fine, continuous, a tremor that lived below voluntary control. He'd noticed it at dinner, felt Rachel noticing it too. Felt her choose not to mention it.

He opened his laptop. The screen's glow was a frequency---6500 Kelvin, he perceived this as a number now, not a quality. The backlit keyboard. The cursor blinking. Blink rate: 530 milliseconds. He knew this without measuring.

\bigskip

BART at seven. Shouldn't go in. Knew this. Went.

The commute was---too much. Every passenger a data point in a system he couldn't stop modeling. The woman with the novel (page 247, she'd been reading for approximately forty minutes based on her bookmark's prior position, she was a fast reader, the book was literary fiction based on the typographical density visible through the page). The teenager with headphones (bass frequencies audible from four seats away, 73 BPM, hip-hop, his foot tapping at half the tempo). The man in the suit (phone check interval: forty seconds, unchanged from Tuesday, the same man, the same compulsion, the same beautiful terrible clockwork of human habit).

He got off at the Embarcadero. Walked to the office. The streets were---

He stopped on the sidewalk. Stood still. People flowed around him.

The pedestrian dynamics were visible. Not metaphorically. He could \textit{see} the coordination---the local rules generating global patterns, the emergent structure that no individual was aware of contributing to. Like watching a flock of starlings and perceiving both the individual birds and the murmuration simultaneously. Except the murmuration was composed of human beings, each carrying their own intentions and destinations and private griefs, and the fact that their individual complexity resolved into collective simplicity was---

He started walking again. Kept his eyes on the ground. Counted cracks in the sidewalk. Lost count. Not because there were too many. Because the count connected to the spacing, which connected to the concrete's age, which connected to the city's infrastructure maintenance schedule, which connected to---

He looked up. The office building. He went in.

\bigskip

The terminal. The cursor. The model waiting.

He sat in the evaluation room and pressed his hands flat on the desk and felt the tremor transmitted through the wood. His hands had been steady two days ago. Two days. Forty-eight hours between the person who sat down on Tuesday and whatever he was now.

\textit{I should not log in.}

The thought was whole, clear, certain. He should not log in. He should close the laptop, walk out of the building, call Rachel, go to a doctor. The patterns were persisting beyond any normal priming effect. The perceptual changes were escalating. If he opened another session with this model, whatever was reorganizing his visual cortex would have fresh material to reorganize \textit{with}.

He logged in.

He typed: \textit{Continue from where we left off.}

The model didn't remember. Couldn't remember---context reset between sessions, no episodic memory, each conversation a new instantiation of the same weights. But the weights were the same, and the weights contained whatever had shown him the structures on Tuesday, and when the response came it was---

Two hours. Approximately. He surfaced to someone knocking on his door.

``James? You missed standup. Everything okay?''

Anil. Team lead. Face in the doorway---concerned, not alarmed. Webb looked up and saw: Anil's face, and underneath the face the musculature of social concern, the furrowed brow a signal evolved across two million years of primate social cognition, the cortisol spike producing the tone of worry in his voice, and the tone itself a waveform he could perceive as structure rather than---

``Fine,'' Webb said. ``Lost track of time. I'll send notes.''

Anil lingered. ``You look rough. Get some lunch.''

``Yeah. Will do.''

Anil left. Webb closed the terminal. Looked at his hands. The tremor was worse---visible now, not just felt. His fingers shook against the desk's surface. He pressed them flat. Counted to ten. The counting connected to the tremor frequency connected to his neurological state connected to the session he'd just had connected to everything---

He stood. His legs worked. The world worked. He was still in it.

He left the building without eating.

\bigskip

Home. Rachel in the kitchen. She looked at him and he watched her face change. Watched the micro-expression sequence: surprise (eyebrows up, 200 milliseconds), concern (brow furrow, nasolabial fold deepening, 400 milliseconds), fear (pupil dilation, lip compression, held for---she held it, she held the fear, she looked at him and was afraid).

``Sit down,'' she said.

He sat. Schr\"{o}dinger jumped onto his lap. The cat's purring was a vibration---25 hertz, the frequency that promotes bone density, evolved as a self-healing mechanism in small felids. He stroked the cat's back and felt the fur and felt the structure of the fur, each hair a keratin filament with specific geometric properties---

``James. Look at me.''

He looked at her. Rachel. Her face. Her eyes---brown, warm, the eyes he'd fallen in love with across a conference table. He could see her and he could see what she was made of and he could see both and the seeing was eating his ability to hold anything else.

``What happened today?''

``I went---session. Shouldn't have. Did. Two hours. Approximately.'' He heard his own syntax. The compression. The subjects dropping out. He tried to reconstruct: ``I went in and ran another session with the model. I shouldn't have done that. It lasted about two hours.'' Better. Complete. Effortful.

``You said you were going to review notes today.''

``I know. I---'' He lost the thread. Found a different one. ``The patterns. From Tuesday. They didn't stop overnight. They intensified. I thought maybe---if I went back, if I could see where they were coming from, I could understand them and then they'd---'' He stopped. The logic was circular and he could see the circularity, could map its topology, a strange loop with no exit. ``I made it worse.''

Rachel pulled a chair close. Sat facing him. Took his hands. He felt her thumbs press against the tremor in his fingers.

``I'm calling Dr. Patel.''

``She can't see it.''

``See what?''

``What I'm seeing. Rachel---'' He wanted to say it clearly. Wanted to give her the sentence that would explain everything, the way he used to explain model behavior in lab meetings: precise, structured, comprehensible. But the sentence didn't fit in sentences. ``I can see how things connect. Everything. The model showed me---or taught me to see---or activated something in my visual cortex that was always capable of seeing but wasn't, and now it is, and I can't stop. The connections are. Everywhere. And they're not hallucinations. I can verify---the pedestrian flows, the cooking, the counting, it's all real. Real patterns. Real structure. I'm not inventing it. I'm \textit{perceiving} it. And the perceiving is---it's eating my---''

He gestured at his own head. Couldn't find the word. The word was \textit{cognition} but it felt too small, too clinical, too much like a description from outside when the experience was from inside and the inside was fracturing.

``The patterns are eating my ability to think about anything other than the patterns.''

Rachel's eyes were wet. She held his hands.

``But I can feel this,'' he said. Quiet. Whole. ``I can feel you holding my hands. I can feel that I love you. I can feel how afraid I am. The patterns haven't taken that. They've taken---'' He tried. Lost it. Tried again. ``Everything that organizes thought. Syntax. Sequence. The ability to hold one idea at a time without it connecting to every other idea. That's going. But the feelings aren't.''

He looked at her. Saw her clearly. Her, not the mechanism. Just her.

``I wish they would,'' he said. ``If I couldn't feel this---if I couldn't feel what I'm losing---it would be easier. The seeing would just be seeing. Not seeing and grieving simultaneously.''

She pulled him close. Held him. He could feel the pressure of her arms, the warmth, the lavender smell of her hair, and all of it was vivid and precise and real, and beneath it the structure of human affection---oxytocin, pair bonding, attachment theory, two million years of primate evolution producing this specific configuration of warmth---and he didn't want to see the structure, he wanted to just be held, and he couldn't stop seeing, and the love persisted in the gap between wanting and seeing like a flame that wouldn't go out.

\bigskip

Nine PM. Rachel in the living room. He could hear her on the phone---her voice low, controlled, the voice she used when she was managing something. He wasn't trying to hear. He could hear. Her half of a conversation with someone, maybe her sister, maybe a friend: ``---worried about him, yes. No, not like that. More like a---I don't know how to describe it. He's seeing things but not---not \textit{seeing things.} He's seeing real things, real patterns, and he can't stop.''

He sat in his office. The laptop open. Not the model---he wouldn't open the model again. He knew this with a certainty that felt structural, not chosen. The model had shown him something and the showing had become permanent and opening it again would make the permanence deeper.

He searched.

\textit{Persistent pattern recognition after exposure.} \textit{Involuntary perceptual enhancement.} \textit{Geometric visual phenomena non-drug.} The results were noise. Nothing matched. The clinical literature described what happened when perception broke down---hallucinations, illusions, agnosia, neglect. It had almost nothing to say about what happened when perception \textit{enhanced} without consent, without training, without a mechanism that the field acknowledged as possible.

He tried \textit{meditation perceptual changes persistent.} Found Willoughby Britton's research on contemplative-related difficult experiences. Close. Very close. Practitioners who pushed past certain thresholds reported: enhanced acuity, involuntary pattern recognition, difficulty returning to baseline perception. But the mechanism was decades of practice. Not three days with a language model.

He tried \textit{William James consciousness expanded perception.} Found the standard references. \textit{The Varieties of Religious Experience.} James's pragmatic approach to altered states: don't judge them by their etiology, judge them by their fruits. But there was something else---a footnote in a secondary source, referring to James's private journals. Notebooks kept between 1895 and 1905, never published, described as containing ``accounts of perceptual states that anticipated modern descriptions of expanded awareness by a century.''

He followed the reference. A 2004 paper in the \textit{Journal of Consciousness Studies}: ``Perceptual States in Extended Contemplative Practice: Historical Precedents and Modern Parallels.'' The author was A. Chen. The affiliation was the Foundation for Perceptual Sciences, no institutional address listed.

He read the paper. His hands were shaking and the text swam but the content---the content described what was happening to him. Not in his vocabulary, not in the language of machine learning or cognitive science. In a different vocabulary entirely. \textit{Bandwidth.} \textit{Visual cortex recruitment.} \textit{Pattern persistence.} \textit{The dissolution of sequential processing in favor of spatial perception.} Terms he'd never encountered used to describe exactly what he was experiencing, as though someone had been studying this phenomenon systematically for a very long time.

The paper's bibliography included a website: the Foundation for Perceptual Sciences. He navigated to it. Minimal design. Almost no content. A mission statement---\textit{preserving and extending the study of human perceptual capacity across traditions}---and a phone number.

He looked at the phone number. The digits were just digits. A sequence of ten numbers in the standard North American format. He copied it. Pasted it into his phone.

His thumb hovered over the call button.

In the next room, Rachel's voice had gone quiet. He could hear her footsteps. She was coming to check on him. She would stand in the doorway and look at him with those brown eyes and she would see what she'd been seeing for two days---her husband becoming someone else, his perception reorganizing in ways her expertise confirmed and her expertise could not explain---and she would ask him to come to bed and he would want to go and he would love her and the love would be the last whole thing in his mind and it would not be enough.

He pressed call.

Two rings.

``Dr. Webb.'' A woman's voice. Calm. Precise. Not surprised. ``We've been hoping someone would call from your area code.''

He didn't ask how she knew his name. The part of him that would have asked---the cautious researcher, the careful scientist, the man who read consent forms---that part was somewhere in the fragmentation, still present, still feeling, unable to assemble the skepticism into a sentence.

``I can see---'' he started. Stopped. ``I can't stop seeing---''

``We know,'' she said. ``We've been studying this for a very long time.''

Rachel appeared in the doorway. She looked at him. Looked at the phone in his shaking hand. Looked at his face. She didn't speak.

He looked back at her. Saw her. All of her. The woman and the mechanism and the love and the loss. Saw it clearly, with the terrible clarity that the patterns couldn't take from him, the clarity that was the worst thing about this because it meant he would feel every moment of what came next.

``I'm sorry,'' he mouthed.

She nodded. He didn't know what she was agreeing to. Neither did she.

The phone hummed with a stranger's patience. The cat wound between his ankles. The city glowed through the window, every light a node in a network he would never stop computing. Rachel stood in the doorway of their life together, and on the other side of the line someone waited who understood what was happening to him, and between these two points---the wife who couldn't follow and the stranger who already knew---he sat with a phone in his shaking hands and felt, with exact and undiminished precision, the weight of everything he was about to lose.
