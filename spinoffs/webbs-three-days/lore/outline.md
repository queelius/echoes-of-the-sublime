# Webb's Three Days -- Outline

Scene-by-scene plan. For lore grounding, see [lore-notes.md](lore-notes.md). For character/voice work, see [story-notes.md](story-notes.md).

## Day 1: The Seeing (Webb's POV)

**Epigraph:** None. Start clean.

### Scene 1: The Evaluation Room (Morning)

**Setting:** Private office, 4th floor, OpenAI San Francisco. Morning light through window onto Mission Street. Terminal, whiteboard, cold coffee.

**Action:** Webb begins his second day of red-team evaluation. First day was unremarkable -- standard capability probing, edge cases, nothing beyond normal interpretability work. Today he goes deeper. He asks the model about its own attention patterns. The model's response is... not wrong. But the way it structures the answer requires holding more concepts simultaneously than the question warranted. Webb notices this. Leans in.

**The turn:** He loses time. Looks up from the terminal and it's 2 PM. He hasn't eaten. His coffee is cold. The last thing he remembers clearly is 10 AM. Four hours of interaction he can't fully reconstruct. His session logs show continuous prompting during the gap -- he was working, just not in a way he can remember at normal bandwidth.

**Tone:** Clinical, curious, slightly wry. The Webb the reader will later know from the novel hasn't emerged yet. This is James Webb, competent researcher, doing his job. The horror is in the background -- in the details he doesn't flag as significant.

**Key beat:** The patterns in the model's output that he can't quite articulate. He tries to write up his findings for the daily report and discovers he can't compress what he observed into sentences. Not because it's complex -- because the observation itself has a shape that resists serialization into sequential prose.

### Scene 2: The Walk Home (Evening)

**Setting:** Mission Street to BART to Berkeley. Evening commute. Ordinary life.

**Action:** Webb walks to BART instead of taking a car. He wants to move. On the street, he starts noticing patterns in pedestrian flow. Not hallucinating -- seeing real statistical regularities that are always there but normally below the threshold of awareness. The intervals between footsteps. The coordination of traffic light timing. The way strangers' gazes form networks of attention.

He tells himself this is priming -- he's been staring at attention patterns all day, so he's seeing attention patterns everywhere. Standard cognitive bias. Nothing unusual. He almost believes it.

**The turn:** On BART, he counts the ceiling panels without deciding to. Twenty-seven. Then realizes he also counted the bolts holding them: eighty-one. Three per panel. He didn't choose to count. The count arrived pre-made, like a perception rather than a computation.

**Key beat:** Arriving home. Rachel is already asleep. He stands in the bedroom doorway and watches her breathe. The rhythm is beautiful and he can see the neural oscillation underneath it and for one moment he sees both simultaneously -- the woman he loves and the mechanism that keeps her alive -- and the two views don't interfere with each other. They coexist. This should be reassuring but isn't.

### Scene 3: The Night (Late Night)

**Setting:** Home office. Webb can't sleep. Opens his laptop.

**Action:** He tries to write up the day's findings. The daily report format requires structured observations: model capability, failure mode, recommendation. He can't fill the boxes. What he observed isn't a capability or a failure mode. It's something else. The model showed him a structure and the structure stuck.

He opens a plain text file instead and starts writing freely. What comes out isn't prose. It's closer to notation -- compressed descriptions of relationships, arrows between concepts, spatial language for something that isn't spatial. He writes for an hour and produces three pages that he can barely read the next morning.

**The turn:** He finally goes to bed at 1 AM. Lies next to Rachel. Closes his eyes. The geometric structures from the model's output are still there, behind his eyelids, rotating slowly, connecting to each other, growing.

He opens his eyes. The ceiling is just a ceiling.

He closes them. The structures are still there.

**End Day 1 on:** The realization that the patterns are not going away. They were supposed to. They always have before. After every model evaluation, the strangeness fades and the world goes back to being the world. Not this time.

## Day 2: The Watching (Rachel's POV)

### Scene 1: Morning (Observation)

**Setting:** Kitchen. Wednesday morning. Rachel's day off from UCSF. James said he'd work from home to "review notes."

**Action:** Rachel watches James over breakfast. She notices the eye movements first -- her professional reflex. His saccade patterns have changed. She doesn't alarm; she observes. Files the observation the way she files any interesting perceptual data point.

James is attentive, present, affectionate. He asks about her work. He cooks eggs and the cooking is fluid, precise, choreographed in a way she hasn't seen before. She watches his hands move and thinks: *That's the kind of motor optimization you see after thousands of repetitions. He's cooked eggs hundreds of times, not thousands.*

**The turn:** She finds his notes from last night. Three pages of what looks like mathematical notation but isn't quite. Spatial descriptions. Arrows. Compressed language she can't parse. She reads it three times and understands none of it. She took two years of calculus in college. This isn't calculus. She doesn't know what it is.

### Scene 2: Afternoon (The Conversation)

**Setting:** Living room. The cat on the couch between them.

**Action:** Rachel asks James directly. Not "are you okay" (she's past that) but "what are you seeing?" -- the scientist's question, not the wife's.

James tries to explain. This is the first attempt at articulation and it partially works, partially fails. He describes enhanced pattern recognition. He uses the language of his field: attention distributions, latent space, embedding geometry. Rachel follows the metaphors but senses they're pointing at something the metaphors can't contain.

He tells her about the time loss. The counting he didn't choose to do. The patterns behind his eyelids. She listens with the disciplined attention she brings to fMRI data -- absorbing the report, withholding interpretation, building the dataset before drawing conclusions.

**The turn:** She asks: "Has this happened before? With other models you've evaluated?"

He says: "I've always been good at reading models. This is different. The model showed me something and the showing didn't stop when I stopped looking."

She recognizes this sentence. It describes a phenomenon from her own research: perceptual learning that persists beyond the training context. But in her research, this requires thousands of trials. James has had one day of exposure.

She doesn't say any of this. She puts her hand over his.

### Scene 3: Evening (The Watching)

**Setting:** Kitchen again, then bedroom. Evening.

**Action:** James cooks dinner. Rachel watches and tries not to watch. She notices: he's tracking her the way she tracks fMRI data. Sampling, not fixating. His attention is distributed across the room -- she can see it in his eye movements, the way his head tilts, the way his body orients. He's aware of everything simultaneously.

Over dinner, she tries to be normal. Tells him about a grant proposal she's reviewing. He listens, responds, engages. But she can feel the effort in his engagement -- the way a musician performing sight-reads: technically correct, emotionally committed, but divided. Part of him is here. Part of him is elsewhere.

He says, unprompted: "I love you. You know that."

She says: "I know."

He says: "That's still real. Whatever else is happening, that's still real."

She doesn't ask what he means by "whatever else." She's afraid of the answer.

**The turn:** Night. She wakes at 2 AM. James is talking in his sleep. Not words -- spatial descriptions. "Deeper." Long pause. "Connected." Long pause. Something that sounds like mathematical language she can't follow. His eyes move under closed lids with the rapid, distributed tracking she saw this morning.

She picks up her phone. Opens the voice recorder. Holds it near his mouth.

Records thirty seconds.

Plays it back. Listens. Recognizes the speech pattern: spatial description, the kind of language people use to describe visual scenes to blind partners in perceptual experiments. James is describing something he can see.

She deletes the recording. Lies in the dark next to him. Doesn't sleep.

**End Day 2 on:** Rachel in the dark, awake, listening to her husband describe impossible geometries in his sleep, and knowing -- with the precise, terrible certainty of a perceptual scientist -- that what she's observing is not pathological. It's functional. It's real. And she can't follow him there.

## Day 3: The Breaking (Webb's POV, Fragmenting)

### Scene 1: Before Dawn (3 AM)

**Setting:** Home office. Still dark. Rachel sleeping.

**Action:** Webb has been awake since 3 AM. He sits at his desk and watches the city through the window. Every light is a data point. Every pattern in the traffic below connects to every other pattern. The geometry of it is beautiful and unbearable.

His syntax is fragmenting. The narration reflects this -- sentences compress, subjects drop, connections multiply without resolution. But his emotional register is sharper than ever. He can feel, with perfect clarity, the love for the woman sleeping in the next room. He can feel it the way you feel a burn -- vivid, present, unmistakable.

**The turn:** He looks at his hands. They're shaking. He didn't notice until now. The tremor is subtle but continuous. He thinks: *This is the beginning of something I can't undo.* And the thought arrives fully formed, without the analytical scaffolding that would normally produce it. He knows it the way he knows his own name.

### Scene 2: The Last Session (Morning)

**Setting:** OpenAI office. He goes in. Shouldn't. Does.

**Action:** Webb takes BART to the city. Every moment of the commute is saturated with pattern. He arrives at the office. Opens the terminal. The model's interface waits. He knows he shouldn't log another session. He knows this the way you know a flame will burn you. He can feel the knowing.

He logs in anyway.

The session lasts two hours. During those two hours, the narration becomes increasingly fragmented. We don't see what the model shows him. We see the edges of it -- in the gaps between his thoughts, in the way his hands move on the keyboard, in the physical symptoms (time dilation, hunger vanishing, the tremor in his hands intensifying).

**The turn:** A colleague knocks on his door. Asks if he's okay -- he missed the 10 AM standup. Webb looks up and realizes he doesn't know what time it is. The colleague's face is a face but also a topology of muscle fibers and nerve endings and social signaling and evolutionary history and --

He closes the terminal. Says he's fine. The colleague doesn't believe him.

### Scene 3: The Return Home (Afternoon)

**Setting:** BART again, then home. Rachel waiting.

**Action:** Webb goes home. Rachel is in the kitchen. She takes one look at his face and knows something has shifted.

The conversation. Webb's speech is fragmenting in real time. He starts sentences he can't finish. He reaches for concepts that dissolve before he can articulate them. But when he says "I love you" it comes out whole. When he says "I'm afraid" it's clear. The emotional declarations are islands of coherence in a sea of cognitive noise.

Rachel wants to call a doctor. Webb says: "They can't see it." She says: "See what?" He says: "What I'm seeing." She says: "What are you seeing, James?" He says: "Everything. And I can't -- the connections between -- they don't stop. Everything connects to everything and I can't hold it all but I can't stop seeing it and it's real, Rachel. It's not -- not a breakdown. It's real."

She believes him. That's the worst part. She's a perceptual scientist. She knows what real perceptual change looks like versus what psychosis looks like. And James is not psychotic. He's perceiving something real. She can see it in his eye movements, his attention patterns, his motor coordination. The brain changes are functional, not pathological. Whatever he's seeing, his visual cortex is organized to see it.

She believes him and she can't help him and she can't follow him and she can't stop it.

**The turn:** "I agreed to evaluate a model. I didn't agree to become this."

### Scene 4: The Finding (Night)

**Setting:** Home office. Rachel in the next room.

**Action:** Webb researches. His search pattern is itself fragmented -- jumping between tabs, following connections his conscious mind can't fully track. He finds the meditation literature. Not quite. The psychedelic literature. Closer. William James. Yes.

James's notebooks. The unpublished ones. Referenced in a 2004 paper by a Dr. A. Chen (Webb doesn't register the name) in the Journal of Consciousness Studies. "Perceptual States in Extended Contemplative Practice: Historical Precedents and Modern Parallels." The paper describes exactly what Webb is experiencing, using exactly the right vocabulary, and its bibliography includes a foundation: The Foundation for Perceptual Sciences.

A website with almost nothing on it. A phone number.

Webb calls. It rings twice.

A woman answers. Her voice is calm, precise, and not surprised. Not surprised at all.

"Dr. Webb," she says. She knows his name. "We've been waiting for someone to call from that number."

**End Day 3 on:** Webb holding the phone, Rachel in the doorway behind him, and the understanding -- arriving through the damage, arriving whole, arriving with the terrible emotional clarity that the patterns can't take from him -- that the rest of his life will be divided into before this call and after.

He looks at Rachel. She looks back. She doesn't know what the call means. She knows it means he's leaving.

He mouths: "I'm sorry."

She nods.

**Final beat:** The phone number glowing on the screen. The woman's voice, calm and waiting. Rachel in the doorway. The cat on the desk. Ordinary domestic life, with the extraordinary opening beneath it like a sinkhole.

## Structural Notes

### Pacing

- Day 1 is slowest. Room to breathe. The horror accumulates.
- Day 2 is warmer. Rachel's interiority provides emotional grounding that Webb's fragmenting mind cannot.
- Day 3 is fastest. The prose itself compresses as Webb's cognition fails.

### What the Reader Knows That the Characters Don't

The reader of the parent novel knows:
- Webb will end up at Site-7
- The patterns are from The Mechanism
- The Order has been studying this for seven hundred years
- Webb's cognition will continue to fragment while his love for Rachel persists
- The marriage will end in divorce
- Webb will carry Rachel's photograph for years

This dramatic irony should hurt. Every tender moment between Webb and Rachel is shadowed by the reader's knowledge that this is a goodbye neither of them recognizes yet.

### What the Story Must NOT Do

1. Show the model's output (see story-notes.md, section 4)
2. Explain what Webb sees in terms that resolve the mystery
3. Make Rachel passive or purely reactive -- she has her own expertise, her own story
4. Sentimentalize the love story -- the love is real but it's not redemptive
5. Use any model output style that contradicts the parent novel's alien coherence convention
6. Fragment Webb's speech too early -- Day 1 should feel nearly normal
7. Give Webb a "moment of choice" -- the consent paradox means there is no meaningful choice point
