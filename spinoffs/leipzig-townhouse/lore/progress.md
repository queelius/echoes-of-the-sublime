### Anachronism Audit (During Drafting)
- Avoided all modern psychological vocabulary ("consciousness" used only as "awareness," "perception" used in the Leibnizian sense)
- No "bandwidth," "pattern-recognition," "cognitive," "neural," "recursive" (modern compound/technical terms)
- Used "Gesichte" (visions) instead of "hallucinations" or "perceptual phenomena"
- "Signs on the paper" instead of "notation" in Blackwood's speech (though "notation" used elsewhere by Leibniz, who coined calculus notation)
- Period-appropriate material culture: beeswax candles, tile stove (Kachelofen), quill pen, perruque, cassock
- Latin phrases for international communication (period-appropriate lingua franca)
- German Umlaut rendered correctly for LaTeX (\"o, \"u, \ss)

### Voice Differentiation Check
- **Leibniz:** Long branching sentences, relational thinking, connecting domains. Formal but with moments of directness when approaching the core. Appropriate for a polymathic mind that perceives connections.
- **Da Sousa:** Careful, bridge-building speech. The diplomat's habit of framing reports for audiences that lack context. Formal German with Jesuit precision.
- **Blackwood:** Short sentences, Latin-default, fear beneath empirical reserve. The Royal Society man experiencing something the Royal Society has no category for.
- **Hesse:** Practical, financial, institutional. Asks "how long" and means it literally.
- **Schreiber:** Precise, archival, anticipatory. Asks questions that reveal she is thinking several steps ahead.

### Structural Notes
- Three sections mirror the Ensho story's three sections but with different content: Arrivals (setup), The Meeting (argument/revelation), The Dawn (aftermath/commitment)
- POV: close third, Leibniz throughout. His branching perception shapes the prose style.
- Single evening temporal span. No flashbacks except Leibniz's narrated memories (Spinoza encounter).
- Closing image: pen moving, ink drying, light growing. The beginning of three centuries of writing that will always be insufficient.

### What Needs Review
- **Blackwood's name**: "Simon Blackwood" is fictional. The character was originally considered as a fictional Halley protege but renamed to avoid confusion with the historical Edmund Halley.
- **Da Sousa's Tibetan timeline**: Historically, Ippolito Desideri (Jesuit) reached Lhasa in 1716, two years after the story. Da Sousa is fictional and his timeline is slightly earlier, which is plausible (Jesuit missionaries traveled through the region before Desideri's formal mission) but should be noted.
- **The Spinoza encounter**: The historical Leibniz really did visit Spinoza in The Hague in November 1676. The coded language and the perceptual content are fictional additions consistent with the novel's lore.
- **The Eckhart manuscript**: Fictional. Eckhart's actual students (Tauler, Suso, the "Friends of God" network) did produce manuscripts, and Augustinian libraries being dissolved in the 18th century is historically plausible.
- **Leibniz's gout**: Historically documented. His health declined in his final years.
