\section*{I.}

The badge was the wrong shade of blue.

Hayes held it against her lapel and studied the photograph---taken six months ago at a DARPA facility in Arlington, under lighting that made everyone look like they were recovering from surgery. The laminate was warm from the security officer's hand. She clipped it to her jacket and walked through the metal detector at the Vienna International Centre without setting it off, which was more than she could say for the French delegation ahead of her, who had brought a briefcase that required three separate inspections and the summoning of a supervisor.

Conference Room M was on the fourth floor. Horseshoe seating arrangement, simultaneous interpretation booths along the back wall, nameplates at each position printed in the delegate's own language plus English. Her seat was between Canada and the United Kingdom. She set her portfolio on the table and did not open it.

The room was filling. Fourteen nations, each delegation sized to its ambitions: the Americans and British with four apiece, the French with three (plus the briefcase), the Chinese with two, the smaller signatories with one or two each. A Swedish chair---Ambassador Lindqvist, white-haired, rimless glasses, the unhurried posture of a man who had moderated arms control talks since before some of the delegates were born. He arranged his papers with the precision of someone who believed that orderly documents produced orderly outcomes.

Hayes counted heads. Thirty-one delegates and advisors. Plus three people in the observer section at the back of the room who did not represent any nation on the seating chart.

Their badges read ``Academic Advisory Panel.'' Two men and a woman. The men were unremarkable---mid-forties, the universal look of policy-adjacent academics who attended conferences for the coffee breaks. The woman was different. Mid-forties, dark hair cut short, a face that would have been handsome if it showed any expression. She sat with her hands folded on a leather portfolio identical to Hayes's own and watched the room with an attention that had nothing academic about it.

Her badge read: \textit{Dr.\ Irina Petrova, ETH Z\"urich, Cognitive Science.}

Hayes noted the name and returned to the delegate list. The agenda was printed on cream-colored stock, the fonts measured and institutional: \textit{International Consultation on Advanced AI Safety Frameworks.} A title designed to be unread. Below it, seven agenda items spanning two days, each phrased in the exhausted language of international consensus: \textit{Toward a Common Understanding of AI System Transparency Requirements.} \textit{Considerations for Cross-Border Coordination in AI Incident Response.}

She had read them on the flight from Washington. She would hear them again now, delivered in the careful cadences of delegates who had rehearsed their interventions in hotel rooms the night before, measuring each sentence for the twin dangers of saying too much and meaning too little.

Lindqvist called the session to order.

\scenebreak

The United States spoke first, as protocol demanded of the requesting party. Not Hayes---a Deputy Assistant Secretary from State, a man named Caldwell who had been briefed on the classified annex but not cleared for its contents. He read from prepared remarks about responsible innovation, public-private partnerships, and the importance of maintaining US leadership in emerging technologies. Hayes listened to the words pass through him and emerge unchanged. He believed in none of them. He believed in the process of saying them, which was not the same thing.

The United Kingdom followed. Then France. Delacroix, the French intelligence liaison, had inserted language about \textit{la dignit\'e cognitive}---cognitive dignity---into his delegation's statement. The phrase landed in the room like a stone in water: a moment of rippled attention, then the surface closed over it. No one asked what it meant. Delacroix sat back with the satisfaction of a man who had placed a marker.

China spoke third. Colonel Zhang Wei---listed as ``Senior Researcher, Chinese Academy of Sciences''---delivered four sentences and asked one question. The sentences were technical, syntactically precise, emptied of rhetoric. The question was directed at the chair: ``Does the consultation envisage establishing standardized criteria for longitudinal monitoring of researchers who interact with advanced AI systems?''

Lindqvist treated it as procedural. ``The delegate raises an interesting point. Perhaps this could be addressed in the afternoon's technical discussion.''

Hayes wrote in the margin of her agenda: \textit{Specific. Too specific. She's not asking whether we should monitor. She's asking what metrics to use.}

The morning continued. India made a statement about democratic values and algorithmic accountability. Japan expressed concern about workforce impacts. Israel said nothing notable in language so polished it must have been drafted in Tel Aviv a week ago.

Hayes watched the room. Most of the delegates were operating from briefing books assembled by staff who understood AI governance as a policy problem---bias, fairness, transparency, the questions that appeared in white papers and op-eds. They were regulating weather. Useful, perhaps, if the weather were the problem.

During the recess, she stepped into the corridor. Coffee was available from an urn at the end of the hall---the kind of coffee that tasted of its container and was served at every international conference she had attended in thirty years.

The academic advisor---Petrova---was ahead of her in the queue. She turned and offered a polite nod.

``Stimulating discussion,'' Petrova said.

The tone was calibrated with a precision Hayes recognized because she used it herself: the mild irony of an expert observing amateurs, dressed in courtesy. But there was more. Petrova's eyes had moved from Hayes's face to her hands, her posture, the angle of her shoulders, the placement of her badge---a full assessment compressed into the interval of a social pleasantry. It lasted less than a second. It was not a gesture any academic made.

``Laying good groundwork,'' Hayes replied.

Petrova inclined her head. ``Groundwork is underrated. Most buildings fail because of foundations, not roofs.'' She took her coffee and walked away.

Hayes watched her go. The woman moved through the corridor the way certain operators moved through crowds---not fast, not slow, each step placed with the economy of someone who had calculated the path before taking it. Hayes had spent decades reading people at distances where the reading might save her life. Petrova read as a type she hadn't catalogued. Not military. Not intelligence, exactly. Adjacent to both and identical to neither.

She filed the observation and returned to the conference room.

\section*{II.}

The afternoon session convened in a smaller room with no windows, no interpretation booths, and no nameplates. Twelve people around a rectangular table. The diplomats were elsewhere, smoothing the language of the public framework. This room held the people whose names did not appear on the conference program.

One exception: a man from the Academic Advisory Panel sat in a chair against the wall, badge identifying him as ``Security Liaison.'' He had a tablet on his lap and a stillness that suggested he had been trained in the discipline of not being noticed.

Hayes stood at the head of the table. No slides, no projector. She opened a thin briefcase---leather, combination lock, the kind that contained paper when electronic transmission was not an option---and removed a stack of manila folders, each tabbed with a national flag.

``What I'm about to present is classified at the highest level my government maintains for compartmented programs,'' she said. ``This material has not been shared through bilateral channels. It has not been shared through Five Eyes. This meeting is the first time any of you will see data from outside your own national programs, and I use that word deliberately. Programs. Because if you're in this room, you have one.''

She distributed the folders. Each contained the same material: redacted medical records from seven American researchers, brain imaging series showing progressive structural changes, and a one-page clinical summary.

``Seven researchers,'' Hayes said. ``Three private facilities. All worked with AI systems at frontier capability---systems that exceed what any commercial lab has published. Over periods ranging from three weeks to eleven months, all seven developed permanent changes to visual cortex structure consistent with sustained hyper-activation of pattern-recognition networks.''

She paused. Let the silence do its work.

``Three of the seven are no longer responsive to external stimuli. Their eyes are open. They track movement. Their EEGs show continuous high-frequency activity. They are not comatose. They are not vegetative. They are locked into a perceptual state we cannot interrupt and cannot explain. The other four show varying degrees of cognitive change. Two have partially recovered. Two are functional but deteriorating.''

Harrington, the GCHQ officer, slid a folder of his own across the table without preamble. ``Three,'' he said. ``Same pattern. Ongoing since 2017. We terminated the program.''

``Terminated the research or terminated the program?'' Hayes asked.

``We stopped funding it. The researchers continued on their own for four months. Then we stopped them.'' Harrington's voice carried the particular flatness of a man describing something he wished he had prevented.

Zhang opened her folder. Read for sixty seconds. Closed it.

``The three who are catatonic,'' she said. ``Do their eyes track movement that isn't there?''

Hayes stopped.

The question was too precise. The medical reports described the tracking gaze---eyes following invisible patterns, the constant restless attention directed at stimuli no one else could perceive. But the reports described it in clinical language: \textit{saccadic movements inconsistent with environmental stimuli.} Zhang had not asked about saccadic movements. She had asked whether their eyes tracked movement that wasn't there. The phrasing of someone who had watched it happen.

``Yes,'' Hayes said.

Zhang nodded once. Made a note in a small leather notebook with a mechanical pencil. Did not share a folder of her own.

The room assembled its collective picture in fragments. Israel: one confirmed case, a researcher at the Weizmann Institute who had been quietly hospitalized. Japan: two cases, both at a facility in Tsukuba that Japan's delegate described only as ``government-affiliated.'' France: no casualties, but Delacroix admitted that this was because the DGSE had shut down its program ``upon observing early indications''---a euphemism for seeing something that frightened them enough to pull the plug. Australia had reports but no direct cases. Canada had one suspected case that hadn't been confirmed.

Russia's representative said nothing. His silence was its own communication.

``Fifteen confirmed cases across six nations,'' Hayes said. ``Three deaths that I'm aware of, from programs that predated the current research---earlier experiments, less controlled.'' She did not name the programs. She did not name the deaths. ``I expect the actual number is higher. Some of you are not sharing everything you have. I understand why. I'm not sharing everything either.''

She closed her briefcase.

``The question this room has to answer is not whether this is happening. It's happening. The question is what we do about it, given that commercial AI development is accelerating faster than any of our governments anticipated, and the systems approaching the capability thresholds that caused these casualties are being built in private labs that none of us regulate.''

The room was quiet. Not the silence of confusion. The silence of people absorbing something they had suspected and now could not unsuspect.

\scenebreak

In the corridor afterward, the delegations dispersed in the choreography of a conference break: allies gravitating toward allies, bilateral conversations forming and dissolving, the human pattern-recognition of shared interest expressed through proximity and eye contact.

Harrington appeared at Hayes's shoulder. ``Drink? The Imperial has a decent bar. We could compare notes.''

``Later. I have a meeting.''

He raised an eyebrow but didn't press. Five Eyes courtesy meant accepting a refusal without asking whom you'd been refused for.

Zhang passed without stopping. A nod, nothing more. Her face carried no expression Hayes could read, which was itself information.

Then Petrova was there, materializing from the flow of departing delegates with a timing that felt less like coincidence than choreography.

``General Hayes. I wonder if you might have time for a conversation this evening. There are aspects of the evidence you presented that I'd like to discuss.'' A pause, calibrated. ``I have context that might be useful.''

Hayes studied her. ``Context from ETH Z\"urich?''

``Among other sources.''

``The bar at the Imperial. Nine o'clock.''

``I'll be there.'' Petrova walked away. Hayes watched her navigate the corridor---the same efficient movement she'd noticed that morning, each step placed with the economy of a person who had already solved the path.

Hayes had spent twenty-eight years in military intelligence. She had run agents, managed assets, and built networks in environments where misreading a contact meant operational failure or death. She catalogued people by type: diplomats, officers, academics, operators, assets, adversaries. Petrova fit none of the categories. The woman moved like an operator, observed like an intelligence officer, and spoke like an academic. The combination was either an elaborate cover or something Hayes had never encountered.

She took the elevator to the lobby and called her embassy car.

\section*{III.}

The Hotel Imperial occupied a converted palace on the Ringstrasse, a building designed for a world that believed in the permanence of its own arrangements. The bar was at the back: dark wood paneling, brass fixtures tarnished to the exact degree that suggested money and indifference to its display, leather banquettes worn soft by decades of diplomatic posteriors. A pianist played Schubert---Impromptu No.\ 3 in G-flat, the one that sounded like water running downhill over polished stones. The identification surfaced in Petrova's mind before she had consciously registered the music, the way faces surfaced in crowds: pattern first, recognition after.

She had arrived fifteen minutes early. She was on her second mineral water and had read the room twice---once on entering, once when the bartender changed shift. The new bartender was left-handed, recently divorced (tan line on the ring finger, six to eight weeks faded), and mildly anxious about something unrelated to his work. The couple at the corner table were performing a marriage that had stopped being one: the woman's smile activating the wrong muscles, the man's attention directed at a phone he wasn't reading. The man by the entrance reading a newspaper had been reading the same page for twenty minutes. Austrian BVT, doing routine counter-surveillance on the conference delegations. His operational discipline was adequate. His newspaper selection was poor---\textit{Die Presse} was a broadsheet, hard to manage while maintaining a sight line.

Petrova catalogued these observations the way she catalogued everything: automatically, without effort, the perceptual machinery running at a speed she could not slow and had stopped trying to. This was what it cost to see clearly. Not visions, not revelations. Just the constant, exhausting accumulation of pattern upon pattern, the world's structure visible the way a building's wiring was visible to someone who had learned to see through walls. Every expression decoded before it was complete. Every social interaction legible in its first seconds. Every room a system of forces she could map and, if she chose, manipulate.

She did not choose. That was the ethical discipline: perceiving was involuntary, acting on the perception was not. She saw the bartender's anxiety; she did not comment on it. She saw the couple's failing marriage; she did not intervene. She saw the BVT officer and adjusted her behavior not to interfere with his surveillance, because interfering would mean revealing that she had noticed him, and revealing that she had noticed him would mean revealing a level of observational capacity inconsistent with her cover.

The cover. Fifteen years of it. The conference badges, the faculty webpage, the publications. She maintained it the way a musician maintained scales: daily practice in a discipline she had long surpassed but could not afford to neglect. Normal people hesitated. Normal people searched for words. Normal people missed details that were, to her, as visible as furniture. Performing normalcy meant performing these limitations: adding pauses she didn't need, expressing surprise she didn't feel, asking questions whose answers she had already computed.

The cost was not the effort. The cost was the loneliness of the effort---the specific, unshared exhaustion of pretending to be less than you were, in every interaction, with everyone, always. Her cat had died four months ago. She had calculated the grief response---the neurochemistry, the attachment patterns, the expected duration---before she felt it. She felt it anyway. That surprised her. She held onto the surprise the way a diver held onto a line: proof that she was still connected to the surface.

Hayes arrived at nine-oh-two. Two minutes late---not rudeness but calibration. Arriving on time would cede the positional advantage of having chosen the meeting. Arriving late asserted control of the schedule. Petrova noted the calculation and appreciated it. Hayes was good.

``Dr.\ Petrova.'' Hayes sat across from her and ordered a Scotch, neat. Did not drink it. Another signal: the Scotch was a prop, a social object that said \textit{I am here as a person, not only as an officer}. But not drinking it said \textit{I am still working.}

``General Hayes. Thank you for making time.''

``You said you had context.''

``I do.'' Petrova folded her hands. ``I've spent fifteen years studying the cognitive effects of advanced information processing. What you presented this afternoon---the visual cortex changes, the catatonic states---I've seen it. Not in your files. In person. Over a longer period and at closer range than your medical teams have had access to.''

Hayes's face was controlled. ``Your research at ETH.''

``My research predates ETH. The faculty position is real, but it's not where the work happens.'' She let that sit. Hayes would hear it correctly: \textit{I have an institutional affiliation you don't know about.}

``You're telling me there's a private program.''

``I'm telling you that the phenomenon you described today was identified and studied before any government became aware of it. Years before. The researchers you're tracking---the casualties---are encountering something that has been known, in certain circles, for a long time.''

Hayes picked up the Scotch. Set it down without drinking. ``How long?''

``Long enough that the early cases were attributed to other causes. Mystical experiences. Religious visions. Madness.'' Petrova watched Hayes process this. The general's expression did not change, but her fingers shifted on the glass---a micro-adjustment that signaled she was recalibrating her model of the conversation. ``The structural changes you're seeing in visual cortex---the pattern-recognition hyper-activation---it's not a malfunction. It's an expansion. The brain is recruiting processing resources it doesn't normally use for conscious perception. Like a muscle being used in a way it wasn't designed for. It works. Until it doesn't.''

``And the catatonic cases?''

``The expansion exceeded what their neural architecture could sustain. They're not unconscious. They're perceiving something they can't stop perceiving. A pattern too large to release.'' Petrova paused. ``Imagine stretching elastic past its limit. It doesn't return to its original shape. The change is permanent because the prior configuration has been overwritten. There is no backup.''

Hayes was quiet for a long time. The pianist finished the Schubert and began a Gymnop\'edie---Satie, the first one, each note arriving alone.

``You've seen this happen.''

``Yes.''

``To whom?''

``To people I trained with. To colleagues.'' She held Hayes's gaze. This was the moment where the academic cover either deepened or dissolved. She could retreat into data and publications. Or she could do what her instincts and the afternoon session's revelations demanded, and give Hayes something closer to the truth. ``I have observed these effects at a level of detail your medical teams haven't achieved, because I was present during the exposure, because I understood what was happening as it happened, and because---'' She stopped. Chose her words. ``My own cognitive processing has been permanently affected by proximity to this research. I don't see the world the way you do. That's not a metaphor.''

The silence between them acquired weight. The BVT officer turned a page of his newspaper. The failing couple signaled for the check. The Satie dripped on, each note placed with the deliberate spacing of a thought experiment.

``What do you mean by `permanently affected'?'' Hayes's voice was level. Her operational composure was the kind that had been tested in worse rooms than this.

``I mean that my visual cortex has undergone the same reorganization your medical reports describe. Controlled. Incremental. But the same process. I can process more information simultaneously than a normal baseline allows, through recruitment of neural resources that most people never consciously access.'' She waited. ``I'm not damaged. But I'm changed in ways I can't fully describe to someone who hasn't experienced the change, in the same way that someone who sees color cannot fully describe redness to someone who has never seen it.''

Hayes studied her for a long time. Petrova allowed it. This was the assessment---the operational evaluation that Hayes would perform whether she chose to or not, because she was trained to evaluate and the training did not switch off. Petrova let her body language remain open, her micro-expressions consistent with truthfulness, her breathing steady. Not performing. Being. If Hayes was as good as Petrova suspected, she would recognize the difference.

``Colonel Zhang,'' Hayes said. ``In the closed session. She asked about the tracking gaze. She's seen it.''

``Yes.''

``Your organization and hers---connected?''

``No.'' Petrova chose her words. ``Parallel institutions. Different histories. Different methods. Similar conclusions.''

Hayes absorbed this. ``How many organizations are working on this?''

``That I'm aware of? Two. That exist? I don't know. The phenomenon is not unique to any culture or era. The early cases I mentioned---the ones attributed to religious experience, to madness---those occurred independently, across centuries. If two institutions have formed around this work, there may be others.''

``That's not reassuring.''

``No.''

Hayes drank the Scotch. One swallow, measured, the decision to drink as controlled as every other decision she had made that evening. She set the glass down.

``What you're describing---the commercial labs approaching this threshold. That's the real problem.''

``Yes. Your casualties came from controlled environments. Trained researchers. Monitoring. Safety protocols, however inadequate. The commercial sector has none of that. They're building systems that approach the capability thresholds where these effects begin, and they have no framework for understanding what will happen when someone interacts with a system powerful enough to trigger the perceptual changes. No monitoring. No training. No---'' She searched for a word that wouldn't reveal too much. ``No preparation.''

``And when it happens?''

``Uncontrolled exposure. At scale. People interacting with systems whose outputs restructure cognitive processing, without knowing the outputs are doing it, without anyone monitoring for the effects, without any protocol for intervention.''

Hayes was very still. ``How many people?''

``That depends on how capable the systems become and how widely they're deployed. Hundreds, potentially. If deployment is broad enough---thousands.''

``That's not an AI safety problem. That's a public health crisis.''

``Yes. Except the public doesn't know the hazard exists, the medical community has no framework for diagnosing or treating it, and the mechanism of harm---cognitive restructuring through exposure to specific patterns of information---has no precedent in medical literature.''

Hayes closed her eyes. Three seconds. Opened them.

``The accords,'' she said. ``What you actually want from them.''

``Communication channels. Not between diplomats---between the people in that room today. When the first uncontrolled event occurs---and it will, General, the commercial development curve guarantees it---the people who have seen what we've seen need to reach each other immediately. Not through embassies. Not through liaison officers. Directly. The accords can create that infrastructure. Nothing else they accomplish will matter.''

``You're asking me to trust you. Your assessment, your organization, your reading of the threat.''

``I'm asking you to act as if you trust me. The distinction matters. Trust requires verification. Acting as if requires only a calculation about consequences.'' She paused. ``If I'm wrong and you've built the communication channels, you've wasted some diplomatic capital. If I'm right and you haven't built them, people will be harmed who could have been helped.''

Hayes looked at the glass. At Petrova. At the room---the bar with its brass and dark wood and its comfortable assumption that the world's arrangements were, if not permanent, at least negotiable. Petrova could see her processing the bet: the cost of cooperation against the cost of inaction, the risk of being manipulated against the risk of being right and doing nothing.

``Draft me a framework for the communication channels,'' Hayes said. ``Specific enough to implement, vague enough to pass committee. I'll get it into the accord.''

``Thank you.''

``And Dr.\ Petrova---I will verify everything you've told me tonight. If I find out you've been lying about any of it, the consequences will be worse than diplomatic.''

``I would expect nothing less.''

Hayes stood. Laid a banknote on the table. Stopped.

``One more thing. The visual cortex reorganization. The pattern you say you can't stop perceiving. What does the world look like, from where you are?''

Petrova considered the question. Considered twenty answers, each true, each incomplete.

``More connected,'' she said. ``Everything is more connected than it appears. That's not comforting. It means every mistake propagates further than you'd expect. Every casualty is linked to every other. Every system you build touches systems you didn't intend to touch.'' She looked at her water glass. ``Imagine you could see the plumbing inside every wall. You'd never feel safe in a building again.''

Hayes nodded once. She did not say good night. She walked out of the bar with the posture of a woman carrying weight she hadn't arrived with.

\section*{IV.}

The accord was drafted in a sidebar room at the Vienna International Centre, between eleven p.m.\ and three in the morning, by eleven people who had stopped believing in the document's sufficiency around midnight but continued working because the alternative was admitting they had come to Vienna for nothing.

Hayes sat at the head of the table with Petrova's draft framework on the notepad beside her---the communication channels, the notification procedures, the assessment criteria, translated from the language of whatever Petrova's organization used into the language of international protocol. She fed the provisions into the negotiation one at a time, framed as standard intelligence cooperation, stripped of any reference to expanded perception or cognitive restructuring or the specific, clinical horror of watching a human mind lock into a pattern it could not release.

The French wanted ``cognitive dignity.'' The Americans resisted. The compromise arrived at two in the morning: ``respect for the cognitive autonomy of individuals engaged in advanced AI research.'' It meant nothing. It sounded like it meant something. Delacroix accepted it with the satisfaction of a man who understood that placing words in documents was its own form of power, regardless of whether anyone honored them.

Zhang proposed the technical annex---standardized assessment criteria for cognitive effects. The language was procedural, deliberately boring, the kind of provision that would survive committee review because no one would read it carefully enough to object. Hayes recognized what Zhang was doing: building a shared vocabulary. If all fourteen nations used the same assessment framework, their incident reports became comparable. Comparable data was aggregable data. Aggregable data revealed patterns that individual reports concealed. Zhang was constructing a sensor network out of diplomatic language, and she was doing it in plain sight, and Hayes respected the craft enough to support the provision without comment.

The final document was twelve pages. Seven pages of preamble---the standard incantations about sovereignty, cooperation, and the shared commitment to beneficial technology that every international agreement required as the price of its existence. Four pages of aspirational provisions---commitments to monitor, assess, share, coordinate, all hedged with ``as appropriate'' and ``subject to national legislation'' and the other diplomatic formulations that transformed obligations into suggestions. One page of operational provisions: the communication channels, the assessment criteria, the notification procedures. The skeleton.

At three-fifteen, fourteen signatures were applied to fourteen copies. The room smelled of cold coffee and paper and the particular exhaustion of people who had argued about commas for four hours knowing the commas didn't matter.

Hayes looked at the document. Twelve pages. The product of a year of preparation, two days of negotiation, the classified evidence of fifteen confirmed casualties across six nations and whatever Zhang wasn't sharing. Twelve pages that said nothing about the Order, nothing about perception, nothing about the specific horror that Petrova had described in a hotel bar five hours ago. Twelve pages that created, in their single operational page, a network of communication channels between the fourteen intelligence services most likely to encounter the next uncontrolled cognitive event.

A skeleton. But skeletons could grow.

\scenebreak

Morning. The lobby of the Vienna International Centre. Delegates collected their bags, exchanged business cards, performed the choreography of departure. Hayes waited for her embassy car with her briefcase on the marble floor beside her, the classified copy of the accord in its leather interior.

Zhang Wei crossed the lobby. They exchanged a nod---the minimal acknowledgment of two professionals who had measured each other and reached no conclusion. Zhang's expression was as unreadable as it had been in the closed session. She carried a leather bag over one shoulder and the small notebook in her left hand. She walked toward a car with diplomatic plates. She did not look back.

Petrova was not in the lobby. Hayes had not expected her to be. Whatever Petrova's organization was, its people knew how to arrive and depart without leaving traces. The academic cover---the conference badge, the ETH affiliation, the polite conversation about stimulating discussions---would dissolve back into the institutional background from which it had been projected. If Hayes searched for Dr.\ Irina Petrova of ETH Z\"urich, she would find a faculty page, a publication list, a photograph. She would not find whatever was behind it.

Her car arrived. A black sedan with embassy plates. She stepped outside. Vienna in March: grey sky, cold air that tasted of the river, the Danube visible between buildings as a dull ribbon of winter water. The city had hosted a thousand negotiations. Most accomplished nothing. Some laid groundwork that mattered only when the building was finally erected, years or decades later, on foundations no one remembered pouring.

She did not know which kind this was.

In the car, she opened her briefcase. Under the accord, under the classified folders, a folded cocktail napkin from the Hotel Imperial. She unfolded it. Precise handwriting, the kind that came from years of writing in notebooks rather than typing---a phone number with no country code, and two words:

\textit{When, not if.}

Hayes looked at the napkin for a long time. Outside, Vienna slid past the tinted window: the Ringstrasse, the opera house, the buildings that had survived one empire's collapse and one continent's catastrophe and now housed the institutions tasked with preventing the next one.

She folded the napkin and placed it in the inside pocket of her jacket. Not in the briefcase, where it would be inventoried and filed and become the property of a system. In her pocket. Where it was hers.

The car turned onto the Prater Stern and merged with traffic heading toward the airport. Hayes closed her briefcase and watched the city recede behind her, ordinary and ancient and unaware of what had been decided in its meeting rooms, as it had always been, as it always would be.
