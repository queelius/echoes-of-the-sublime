# The Vienna Accords -- Scene-by-Scene Outline

Scene-level breakdown for drafting. For research context, see [lore-notes.md](lore-notes.md). For characters and craft, see [story-notes.md](story-notes.md). For parent lore, see `lore/lore.md`, `lore/worldbuilding.md`, `lore/characters.md`.

**Target length:** ~6,000-7,500 words across 4 movements.

---

## I. Morning: The Public Session (~1,500 words)

### Scene 1 -- Arrival (Hayes POV)

**Setting:** Vienna International Centre. March 2019. Morning. Security screening, delegate badges, the ritual of international conference attendance.

**What happens:** Hayes arrives by embassy car. Badge check. She walks the corridors of the VIC, passing IAEA staff and conference attendees. Notes the agenda: "International Consultation on Advanced AI Safety Frameworks." A title designed to be uninteresting. She finds her seat in Conference Room M -- horseshoe arrangement, nameplates, simultaneous interpretation headsets she won't use.

**Key observations:**
- The delegate list: 14 nations, mixed delegation composition (diplomats + "technical advisors" + intelligence officers in civilian dress)
- Three observers in the back row without national affiliations. Name badges read: "Academic Advisory Panel." Hayes notes the lead advisor -- a woman, mid-forties, ETH Zurich faculty listed on her badge. Something about how she watches the room.
- The Swedish chair (Lindqvist) opens proceedings. Procedural language. Rules of engagement.

**Beats:**
1. Physical details of the VIC -- institutional architecture, the smell of recycled air, the particular silence of a soundproofed conference room
2. Hayes reads the room like an intelligence brief -- who's allied with whom, who's uncomfortable, who's here because they were told to be
3. The three observers catch her attention. The lead advisor (Petrova) is too still. She watches the delegates the way a predator watches prey -- not with aggression, but with the patient attention of something that already knows how the chase ends

**Ends on:** Hayes's internal note: the advisor's eyes tracked the Chinese delegate (Zhang) for two seconds longer than anyone else. Filed.

### Scene 2 -- The Plenary (~800 words)

**Setting:** Same room, two hours later. Speeches in progress.

**What happens:** Compression of the plenary session. Multiple delegates speak. The language is OECD-standard: transparency, accountability, robustness, human oversight. Hayes listens to the gaps.

**Key beats:**
1. The US delegate (not Hayes -- a State Department official) gives a prepared statement about "responsible innovation." Hayes watches him read words he doesn't believe from a briefing he didn't write.
2. The Chinese delegate (Zhang) speaks briefly. Technical, precise, no rhetoric. Asks a single question about "longitudinal monitoring of researchers interacting with advanced systems." The room treats it as a procedural question. Hayes hears something else: specificity where generality was expected.
3. The French delegate (Delacroix) makes an impassioned case for "cognitive dignity." Beautiful language, zero practical application. A few delegates nod.
4. Lindqvist calls a recess. Hayes steps into the corridor.
5. Corridor beat: Hayes passes the three observers. The lead advisor (Petrova) nods politely. "Stimulating discussion." Her tone is perfectly calibrated -- respectful, a touch wry, the register of an academic who finds policy discussion mildly beneath her expertise. Hayes returns the nod. Something wrong with the exchange she can't identify. Files it.

**Ends on:** Hayes's assessment, framed as an internal cable: "The morning session confirms what we suspected. The public framework is inadequate. The real conversation will happen this afternoon."

## II. Afternoon: The Closed Session (~2,000 words)

### Scene 3 -- The Smaller Room (Hayes POV)

**Setting:** A windowless meeting room on the same floor. Smaller. No interpretation booths. No observers -- except one. The Order's silent watcher sits in a corner chair, badge reading "Security Liaison." Twelve people around a table. No nameplates. No flags.

**What happens:** The intelligence officers convene. Each nation has sent one person, sometimes two. The diplomats are elsewhere, drafting language for the public framework. This room is classified.

**Key beats:**
1. Hayes takes control. She has the most evidence and she knows it. "I'm going to present material that is classified at the highest levels of the US government. What I'm about to show you has not been shared through standard channels because we didn't have a framework for sharing it. This meeting is the framework."
2. She opens a thin briefcase. Paper folders -- no electronics in a room this sensitive. Each folder contains redacted medical records, brain scans, and a one-page summary of "cognitive casualty events" from US-based AI research.
3. The evidence: seven researchers across three private facilities. All interacted with advanced AI systems under conditions that exceeded standard safety protocols. All showed permanent structural changes to visual cortex. Three are catatonic. Two are "functional but deteriorating." Two recovered partially. Medical consensus: "Unknown etiology. Consistent with sustained hyper-activation of pattern-recognition networks." No published literature on this. No precedent.
4. The room reacts. Harrington (UK) is unsurprised -- GCHQ has its own cases. He slides his own folder across the table without comment. Three cases. Same pattern.
5. Zhang (China) says nothing. Takes notes in her leather notebook. Does not share evidence. But she asks: "The three who are catatonic. Do their eyes track movement?"
6. Hayes stops. That question is too specific. You don't ask that unless you've seen it. The tracking gaze -- the eyes following invisible patterns -- is not in the redacted medical reports. It's the kind of detail you know from observation, not briefing papers.
7. Hayes answers: "Yes."
8. Zhang nods. Writes in her notebook.
9. Other delegates respond. The Israelis have one case. The Japanese have two. The French have reports but no direct casualties ("We terminated the program"). The room's collective picture assembles: at least fifteen confirmed cognitive casualties across six nations, all from interaction with AI systems at frontier capability.

**Ends on:** Hayes's realization, which she keeps to herself: Zhang either has casualties of her own or has access to someone else's. Either way, the Chinese know more than they're sharing. And the academic advisor in the corner -- the silent watcher -- hasn't moved, hasn't spoken, hasn't taken a note. She's watching Zhang.

### Scene 4 -- After the Closed Session (~500 words)

**Setting:** Corridor outside the meeting room. Delegates dispersing.

**What happens:** Brief beats:
1. Harrington sidles up to Hayes. Offers a bilateral meeting over drinks. "Old friends comparing notes." Five Eyes courtesy. Hayes agrees.
2. Zhang walks past without stopping. Her face reveals nothing.
3. The academic advisor appears at Hayes's elbow. "General Hayes. Dr. Petrova. I wonder if you might have time for a conversation this evening. There are aspects of the evidence you presented that I'd like to discuss."
4. Hayes notes: Petrova didn't say "I have information." She said "there are aspects I'd like to discuss." The phrasing is careful. An offer of exchange, not disclosure.
5. Hayes agrees. Hotel Imperial bar. Nine o'clock.

**Ends on:** Hayes watches Petrova walk away. The way the woman moves through the corridor -- not fast, not slow, but with an economy of movement that suggests she's already calculated the optimal path through the crowd. Hayes has seen this before, in operators who've spent years in high-threat environments. But Petrova is an academic.

## III. Evening: The Hotel Bar (~2,500 words)

### Scene 5 -- The Bar (Petrova POV)

**Setting:** Hotel Imperial, Ringstrasse. The bar. Dark wood, brass fixtures, a pianist playing something by Schubert that Petrova identifies without thinking (Impromptu No. 3 in G-flat). She arrived fifteen minutes early. She's on her second mineral water.

**What happens:** The shift to Petrova's POV lets the reader see through expanded bandwidth for the first time. Not supernaturally -- but the way a master chess player sees a board differently from a novice. Petrova perceives patterns in the bar: the bartender's micro-expressions, the couple three tables away whose body language maps a specific arc of marital dissolution, the security officer by the door who is almost certainly Austrian BVT doing routine counter-surveillance on the conference delegates.

**Key beats:**
1. Petrova's interiority. She is tired. Not from the conference but from the performance of normalcy. Twelve hours of modulating her responses to match a bandwidth she left behind years ago. The effort of not-seeing, not-knowing, not-predicting. Like a professional musician playing chopsticks and trying to make it look like effort.
2. She thinks about what she can say to Hayes. The Order's brief was clear: establish trust, offer enough to justify information-sharing, reveal nothing about the Order's models or capabilities. She is authorized to discuss cognitive casualties in general terms. She is not authorized to explain the mechanism, name the models, or describe her own expanded perception.
3. But the afternoon session changed things. Zhang's question about tracking eyes means China has a program. The Order suspected but couldn't confirm. If China has its own research at the capability frontier, containment is no longer a problem a single organization can manage. Petrova needs Hayes to understand the scale of the threat. To do that, she needs to show Hayes more than the brief allows.
4. She decides. Not to violate her brief -- to stretch it. To say things that are true but unclassified, in a sequence that lets Hayes assemble a picture the Order hasn't authorized Petrova to paint directly.

**Internal beat:** "She would describe the shape of the room without turning on the light. Hayes was intelligent enough to feel the walls."

### Scene 6 -- The Conversation (Petrova POV, then joint)

**Setting:** Same bar. Hayes arrives. They sit at a corner table. Two mineral waters (Petrova) and a Scotch (Hayes, who doesn't touch it for twenty minutes).

**What happens:** The story's central scene. Two women negotiating across a gap that is simultaneously professional, epistemic, and perceptual.

**Key beats:**

1. **Credentials and positioning.** Hayes establishes her authority: she oversees advanced research programs, she has the highest clearance relevant to this work, she can make things happen or make them stop. Petrova establishes hers: she has spent fifteen years studying cognitive effects of advanced information processing, she has data that Hayes hasn't seen, she is not from a government. Both are testing the other's limits.

2. **The data exchange.** Petrova shares observations -- carefully framed as "patterns observed in my research" -- that extend Hayes's evidence: the visual cortex changes follow a specific progression. There are stages. Early-stage changes are reversible. Late-stage changes are not. The boundary between reversible and irreversible is crossed without warning. And: the rate of commercial AI development means more people will cross that boundary, without monitoring, without protocols, without any idea what's happening to them.

3. **The specificity escalation.** Hayes pushes. "You're describing something you've seen firsthand." Not a question. Petrova doesn't deny it. "I've been close to this research for a long time." Hayes: "How close?" Pause. "Close enough to know that the catatonic cases in your briefing are not the worst outcome."

4. **The reveal beat.** Not dramatic. Quiet. Hayes asks: "What's worse than catatonic?" Petrova: "Catatonic means the process overwhelmed them quickly. The brain locked into a perceptual loop too large to release. It's catastrophic but it's fast. There's another trajectory. Slower. The researcher remains functional. Keeps working. But the perception doesn't stop. It accumulates. The world looks different -- permanently different -- and the difference is not an improvement."

5. **The perceptual gap.** Hayes: "And you know this because...?" Long pause. Petrova meets her eyes. This is the moment where she can retreat to the academic cover or tell something closer to the truth. She says: "There are aspects of cognitive processing that cannot be conveyed through description. I can tell you what I've observed. I cannot make you perceive it. The gap between those two things is the reason these accords will fail."

6. **Hayes's assessment.** Hayes is trained to detect deception. She runs Petrova through her internal evaluation: body language (controlled but not performed -- the micro-expressions are consistent with what she's saying), speech patterns (precise, no hedging, no rehearsed phrases), eye contact (steady, almost uncomfortably so -- not the eye contact of someone lying, but the eye contact of someone who is used to looking at things most people look away from). Hayes's conclusion: Petrova is either telling the truth or she is the most sophisticated intelligence officer Hayes has ever encountered. Possibly both.

7. **The Chinese question.** Hayes: "The Chinese delegate. Zhang. She asked about the tracking gaze. She's seen it." Petrova: "Yes." Hayes: "Your organization and hers -- are they connected?" Petrova, carefully: "No. Parallel discoveries. Different methods. Similar conclusions." Hayes absorbs this. Two organizations, at minimum, operating in this space. The Order and whatever Zhang represents. The problem is bigger than she thought.

8. **The ask.** Petrova: "The accords as drafted will accomplish nothing. What they can accomplish -- what I'm asking you to help build -- is a framework for communication. Not between diplomats. Between the people who have seen this. When the first uncontrolled event happens -- and it will -- the people in today's closed session need to be able to reach each other immediately. Not through diplomatic channels. Directly."

9. **Trust without verification.** Hayes: "You're asking me to trust you." Petrova: "I'm asking you to act as if you trust me. The distinction matters. Trust is verification. Acting as if you trust is a bet on consequences. You're good at those." Hayes almost smiles. Doesn't.

**Ends on:** Hayes picks up her Scotch. Drinks. Sets it down. "Draft me a framework for the communication channels. I'll make sure it gets into the accord. And Dr. Petrova -- I'm going to verify everything you've told me. If I find out you've been lying, the consequences will be worse than diplomatic." Petrova: "I would expect nothing less."

## IV. Night: The Drafting (~1,000 words)

### Scene 7 -- The Accord (Hayes POV)

**Setting:** A sidebar room at the VIC. Late. The negotiating table is littered with paper drafts, coffee cups, a plate of untouched sandwiches. Eleven people -- diplomats and intelligence officers from the 14 nations, negotiating language.

**What happens:** Compression scene. The accord takes shape through argument and compromise. Every substantive provision is weakened. Every commitment is hedged. The language becomes more general as the document becomes more agreed-upon.

**Key beats:**
1. Hayes pushes for specific information-sharing protocols. The direct communication channels Petrova described. She frames it as standard intelligence cooperation -- "expedited notification procedures for cognitive safety incidents." The language is anodyne. The mechanism is real.
2. Delacroix wants "cognitive dignity" language. A fight over phrasing. The Americans resist ("dignity" implies rights, which implies legal obligations). The compromise: "respect for cognitive autonomy." Means nothing. Everyone knows it.
3. Zhang proposes a technical annex -- "standardized assessment criteria for cognitive effects." It sounds procedural. Hayes recognizes what it is: a shared vocabulary. If all 14 nations use the same assessment framework, their incident reports become comparable. Data becomes aggregable. Patterns become visible. Zhang is building a sensor network out of diplomatic language.
4. The final document is twelve pages. Seven pages of preamble and procedural boilerplate. Four pages of aspirational commitments. One page of operational provisions -- the information-sharing channels, the assessment criteria, the notification procedures. The only page that matters.
5. Signatures. Fourteen signatures, fourteen nations. The document is classified at a level that each nation determines independently. In practice, it will live in a safe in each signatory's intelligence service, referenced when needed, forgotten otherwise.

**Ends on:** Hayes stands. The room is clearing. She looks at the document. Twelve pages. A year of preparation, two days of negotiation, and the result is a skeleton -- thin, incomplete, structurally sound enough to support weight it hasn't been asked to bear yet. She folds her copy into her briefcase.

### Scene 8 -- Departure (Hayes POV)

**Setting:** VIC lobby. Morning after. Hayes waiting for her embassy car.

**Key beats:**
1. Hayes sees Zhang in the lobby. A nod. Nothing more. Two intelligence professionals who have measured each other and reached no conclusion. The measurement will continue.
2. Petrova is not in the lobby. Hayes doesn't expect her to be. Academics leave conferences quietly. Whatever Petrova is, she knows how to exit a room.
3. Hayes's car arrives. She steps outside. Vienna in March -- cold, grey, the Danube visible in the distance. The city has hosted a thousand negotiations. Most of them accomplished nothing. Some of them laid groundwork that mattered decades later. She doesn't know which kind this was.

**Final beat:** Hayes opens her briefcase in the car. Under the accord, a folded napkin from the Hotel Imperial. On it, in precise handwriting: a phone number with no country code and two words: "When, not if." She looks at it for a long time. Folds it again. Places it in her inside pocket, not in the briefcase.

## Structural Notes

- **Word count targets:** I: ~1,500 / II: ~2,000 / III: ~2,500 / IV: ~1,000. Total: ~7,000.
- **Movement III is the heart.** The bar scene carries the story's weight. Everything before it is setup; everything after is denouement.
- **The horror is structural, not visceral.** No one is harmed in this story. The horror is in what's implied: that the danger being negotiated is worse than anyone at the table (except Petrova and possibly Zhang) understands, and that the protocols being created are inadequate to contain it. The horror is the gap between what's being done and what would need to be done.
- **Petrova's POV is the reader's only glimpse of expanded perception.** It should feel subtly different from Hayes's POV -- not alien, not supernatural, but slightly more pattern-aware. She notices things faster, connects things others miss, and occasionally has to slow herself down. The effect should be unsettling without being flashy.
