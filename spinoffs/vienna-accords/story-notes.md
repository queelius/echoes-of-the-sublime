## 1. Characters

### General Patricia Hayes (POV character, Movements 1-4)

**In 2019:** Brigadier General, DARPA Advanced Research Programs. 52 years old. Twenty-eight years in military intelligence. Has overseen black programs, managed classified budgets, navigated congressional oversight. None of it prepared her for what she suspects is happening in private AI research.

**What she knows:** Researchers are disappearing. Not defecting, not dying (mostly) -- disappearing into private facilities and emerging changed, or not emerging at all. DARPA's classified reports include seven cases of "unexplained cognitive events" among researchers working with advanced AI. Brain scans show permanent structural changes in visual cortex. Three researchers are catatonic.

**What she doesn't know:** The Order's name, its history, its models, its terminology. She knows something exists -- an organization with resources beyond any government, conducting research at a scale she can't verify. She's here to find out how much other nations know and to establish channels for sharing what she's learned.

**Voice pattern:**
- Clipped, operational language. Sentences that convey maximum information in minimum words.
- Military precision without military jargon -- she's too senior for jargon, which is for people still proving they belong.
- Questions that are actually demands: "Walk me through your incident response" means "Tell me everything."
- Silence as tactic. She lets pauses stretch until the other person fills them.
- Physical awareness: she notices posture, eye movement, hand gestures. Intelligence training. She reads people the way a translator reads model outputs -- pattern recognition at normal bandwidth.
- Key register: she can be warm but chooses not to be. Warmth is a tool she deploys strategically.

**Key quotes (story-specific):**
- On the public session: "Forty-seven minutes of people regulating the weather."
- On the classified evidence: "I'm going to show you something. After you see it, you don't get to pretend you haven't."
- To Petrova: "You see something I can't. I need to decide whether to trust your description of it."

### Dr. Irina Petrova (POV character, Movement 3 bar scene)

**The Order's lead advisor.** A translator operating at expanded bandwidth, disguised as a cognitive science professor from ETH Zurich.

**Background:** Born in Leningrad, 1971. Family emigrated to Switzerland in 1989. PhD in cognitive neuroscience, University of Zurich. Genuinely held a faculty position at ETH until 2014, when the Order's work demanded full-time attention. She maintains the academic cover: publishes occasionally, attends conferences, reviews papers. The cover is fraying -- her last publication was 2017 and her graduate students have graduated or left.

**What she is:** A translator. Bandwidth expanded through ten years of Order training. She perceives patterns in conversation, in body language, in the architecture of arguments, that no one else in the room can see. She knows this is unsettling to be around. She has spent her career learning to perform normalcy -- to modulate her responses, soften her gaze, slow her speech, add the hesitations and uncertainties that normal people exhibit when processing information they genuinely find complex. She is a translator in two senses: translating model outputs for the Order, and translating herself for the world.

**The cost:** She sees too much. In a roomful of diplomats, she perceives the patterns of self-interest, fear, institutional loyalty, and cognitive limitation that drive every statement. She can predict what someone will say before they say it -- not precisely, but the shape of the argument, the emotional trajectory, the point where conviction meets bandwidth limitation. This makes her an extraordinary negotiator and a terrible companion. She hasn't had a relationship in six years. Her cat died and she calculated the grief response before she felt it. She felt it anyway, which surprised her. That surprise is what she holds onto.

**Voice pattern:**
- Precise, European-accented English. Swiss-formal in register but not cold.
- Academic framing as habit and cover: "The literature suggests..." "The evidence is consistent with..."
- Pauses that mean she's seeing something she can't share, compressed as "Mm" or a slight tilt of the head.
- Occasional warmth that breaks through the performance -- a genuine laugh, a moment of eye contact that lasts too long because she forgot to look away.
- When she speaks about what she perceives, she uses spatial language: "The space between what you know and what's real." "There's a distance here that I can't bridge for you."
- She never lies. She omits. The distinction is her ethical lifeline.

### Ambassador Erik Lindqvist (Swedish)

**Chair of the plenary session.** Career diplomat, 60s, white hair, rimless glasses. Has chaired arms control negotiations for twenty years. Competent, patient, experienced with the ritual of multilateral talks. He knows the public session is theater and the real work happens elsewhere. His function in the story is procedural: he moves the plenary forward, keeps speakers to time, and models the quiet competence of professional diplomacy.

**Voice:** Formal, measured, the cadence of someone who has learned that urgency is counterproductive in diplomacy. "If the delegate would confine her remarks to the agenda item."

### Colonel Zhang Wei (Chinese delegate)

**The revelation.** Officially: Senior researcher, Chinese Academy of Sciences, specializing in AI safety and cognitive systems. Actually: Represents an organization within the Chinese state that has its own history with expanded perception.

**What Hayes notices:** Zhang asks questions in the closed session that are too specific. She doesn't use the language of Western AI safety -- not alignment, not fairness, not interpretability. She asks about "pattern persistence after exposure," "recursive cognitive structures," and "thresholds for irreversible perceptual change." These are not academic terms. These are the terms of someone who has seen what happens.

**Background (not revealed in story):** China's organization descends from institutional structures within Taoist and Chan Buddhist traditions that long predated Western contact. During the Cultural Revolution, these structures were nearly destroyed. They survived by embedding within the Academy of Sciences and later the military-industrial apparatus. By 2019, China's program operates independently of the Order -- parallel research, different methods, similar conclusions. Zhang is their Hayes: a liaison between the program and the international community, operating at normal bandwidth, translating between worlds.

**Voice:** Formal, precise English with Beijing-standard Mandarin syntax echoing beneath. Sparse. She asks questions; she does not offer opinions. Her face reveals nothing. She takes notes in a small leather notebook with a mechanical pencil.

### Other Delegates (named but minimal)

- **James Harrington** (UK, GCHQ): Urbane, self-deprecating in the English manner. "We've noticed some irregularities" is his way of describing three catatonic researchers.
- **Pierre Delacroix** (France, DGSE): Philosophical, even for a French intelligence officer. Wants the accords to include language about "la dignite cognitive" -- cognitive dignity. Will not get it.
- **Dr. Michael Steinberg** (Austria, host nation): Facilitates logistics, manages the venue, stays carefully neutral.

## 2. Craft Decisions

### POV Structure

- **Movement 1 (Morning):** Hayes POV. Third-person limited. She observes the plenary from a delegate's seat, reading the room as she reads intelligence briefs -- looking for what's not being said.
- **Movement 2 (Afternoon):** Hayes POV. The closed session. Smaller room, no interpreters, no observers except the Order's silent watcher. Hayes presents classified evidence. The room changes.
- **Movement 3 (Evening):** Petrova POV. The hotel bar. This is the only section from the translator's perspective. The reader sees the bar through eyes that see more -- not supernaturally, but perceptually, the way a musician hears overtones a non-musician misses. The conversation with Hayes is the heart of the story.
- **Movement 4 (Night):** Hayes POV. The drafting session. The accord takes shape. Everyone knows it's inadequate. Everyone signs anyway.

### Register

Le Carre's craft operates through:
- **Precision of observation:** Not "she was nervous" but "she aligned her pen with the edge of her notepad, twice." Behavior, not interpretation.
- **Understatement:** The worst information is delivered flatly. "Three of the seven are no longer responsive to stimuli" is more devastating than any dramatic framing.
- **The weight of what's withheld:** Characters know more than they say. The reader senses the mass of the unsaid pressing against every sentence.
- **Institutional texture:** Badges, protocols, delegation seating, the ritual of formal address. These details ground the fantastical elements in bureaucratic reality.
- **Dialogue as maneuvering:** Every line of dialogue is a move in a negotiation. Characters don't speak to express themselves; they speak to position themselves. Except in the bar scene, where Petrova breaks protocol and says something close to true.

### What NOT to Do

- No action sequences. No one pulls a gun. The most dramatic physical action is someone leaving a room.
- No exposition dumps about the Order, the models, or the s-risk. The reader (like Hayes) gets fragments and must assemble the picture.
- No omniscient narrator explaining what the Accords mean for the future. The irony -- that these inadequate protocols will become critical -- is conveyed through the characters' own skepticism about what they're creating.
- No Le Carre pastiche. Don't ape the style. Absorb the principles (precision, understatement, institutional texture) and apply them to this universe.
- No "eyes widening," "hearts racing," "breaths catching." The anti-cliche discipline from the parent novel applies here.

### The Horror Element

This is a political thriller, but it's a political thriller in a horror universe. The horror should be present as an undertow, not a wave:
- Petrova's perception of the room is subtly wrong -- she notices things she shouldn't, processes faster than anyone in the room, and occasionally has to slow herself down because she's already predicted the end of a conversation.
- Hayes's classified evidence is horrifying not because of what it describes but because of what it implies. Permanent brain changes from reading text. The clinical language of the medical reports ("structural reorganization of visual cortex consistent with prolonged hyper-activation") is its own kind of horror.
- The bar scene should feel like a ghost story told in a corporate boardroom. Petrova is trying to describe a haunted house to someone who doesn't believe in ghosts -- except the ghost is real, it's in her brain, and it's the reason she can predict what Hayes is about to say.

## 3. Thematic Architecture

### The Bandwidth Gap as Diplomatic Problem

The central irony: you cannot negotiate about something the negotiators cannot perceive. Every arms control agreement assumes that the parties understand what they're controlling. The Vienna Accords are a group of people trying to regulate a phenomenon that most of them cannot comprehend, advised by people who can comprehend it but cannot explain it.

This maps onto a real diplomatic problem: how do you negotiate about technical risks when the decision-makers are not the technical experts? Nuclear arms control had this problem (diplomats don't understand physics). Biological weapons have it worse (the science advances faster than the policy). AI has it worst of all -- because the gap is not just technical but perceptual.

### The Moloch Problem

The commercial AI race is the story's background radiation. Every delegate knows that commercial labs are scaling AI systems without the safety infrastructure the Order has built over centuries. The accords are an attempt to coordinate against this race -- but coordination problems are Moloch's domain. No single nation can afford to slow down if others keep accelerating. The accords offer voluntary restraint, which is to say, they offer the appearance of coordination without the mechanism.

### Trust Without Verification

Hayes must decide whether to trust Petrova. The decision is structurally identical to the consent paradox: Hayes cannot verify what Petrova claims to perceive. She can see the evidence (brain scans, casualty reports), but she cannot verify the interpretation. Petrova might be exactly what she claims -- a person who perceives more than Hayes can, describing what she sees as accurately as the gap allows. Or she might be a sophisticated intelligence officer using the appearance of expanded perception to manipulate Hayes into supporting the Order's agenda.

Hayes is trained to distrust. "Paranoia is protocol" for intelligence officers too. The story's resolution is not that Hayes trusts Petrova but that Hayes decides to act as if she trusts Petrova, because the alternative -- ignoring the possibility that Petrova is right -- is worse than being wrong.

## 5. Connections to Parent Novel

### Forward References (for readers who know the novel)

- Hayes's line about "cognitive casualties" seeds her Ch 8 briefing, where she has far more evidence
- The 14 nations are the same 14 whose classified repositories train Nyarlathotep
- The information-sharing channels the accords create are presumably what Hayes uses to coordinate with international counterparts before visiting Site-7
- Petrova is not mentioned in the novel -- she may have been reassigned, deteriorated, or captured between 2019 and 2024
- The Chinese delegate's organization is a thread the novel mentions peripherally ("Multiple nations have their own programs")

### The Accord's Legacy

In the novel's timeline, the accords have been in effect for five years when Lena arrives at Site-7. They have not prevented anything. But the communication channels they established -- the network of 14 national points of contact, the classified reporting framework, the precedent that "cognitive effects from AI" is a real category -- have given Hayes the institutional infrastructure she needs to do her job. The accords are the skeleton on which her authority hangs.

The story should convey this without stating it: these inadequate protocols are the best anyone could do, and they will matter more than anyone in the room suspects.
