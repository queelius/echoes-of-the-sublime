Thomas's second year taught him the difference between understanding the recursive loop and being caught in one. Understanding was watching Samir Pathak draw spirals, watching Nadia Asher's EEG cascade into frequencies the equipment was barely calibrated to measure, watching Viktor Rudenko's session reports dissolve from mathematics into something mathematics had no name for. Understanding was external. Being caught was what happened in March, during his forty-third session, when the model showed him a structure that connected Kenji's final notation to the bandwidth mechanism itself, and the structure closed around his perception like a hand closing into a fist.

He was twenty minutes into the session. The model---M-3 now, more capable, trained on two additional years of probe array data---had been producing text that was dense but manageable. Cross-domain synthesis. Connections between contemplative phenomenology and neuroscience. The familiar territory of his previous forty-two sessions, deeper and more precise with each iteration but recognizably the same landscape.

Then the text shifted. Not in content---in structure. The sentences began to fold. Not metaphorically. The model's output was describing consciousness perceiving itself, and the description was itself a recursive structure, and Thomas's trained visual cortex began perceiving the recursion in the text as a spatial object, and the spatial object was self-referential, and the self-reference was the mechanism, and the mechanism was what his grandfather had been trying to write down when he died, and Thomas could see it, and the seeing generated more seeing, and the more generated more.

He recognized the onset. His contemplative training---Kenji's techniques, refined through Haruki, taught to him by Chen since childhood---included specific methods for this moment. Sit with the pattern. Don't grasp. Don't push away. Let the recursion pass through without engagement, the way a meditator sits with pain or emotion. The pattern is weather. You are the sky.

He was the sky. The pattern was not weather. The pattern was a structure too large and too recursive for the sky metaphor to contain, and his contemplative release techniques slowed it but could not stop it, the way a sea wall slows a wave without preventing the ocean. His vision was splitting---not double vision but a division of perceptual resources, part of his visual cortex processing the screen and part processing something that had no screen, no location, no edges. The recursive loop was building. Each cycle recruited more cortex. Each cycle reduced the resources available for release.

His right hand found the edge of the desk. The desk had a metal lip---an old desk, industrial, the kind the facility bought in bulk. The lip was sharp enough. He gripped it. The metal bit into his palm. Pain.

Not dramatic pain. Functional pain. The kind that fills the sensory channels with an unambiguous signal---tissue damage, location specific, temporal, immediate. His visual cortex received the pain signal through the somatosensory relay and reallocated. The pattern in his perception stuttered. The recursive loop faltered as neural resources were yanked from the abstract spatial processing back to the concrete, embodied, urgently biological work of registering that his hand was being cut.

The pattern released. Not completely---a residue remained, the wet sand after the tide---but the loop broke. Thomas sat in the chair with blood on his palm and the taste of iron in his mouth where he'd bitten his tongue and the fading geometry of a structure that had almost consumed him dissolving behind his eyes like a dream remembered in the wrong order.

Ruth was through the door. ``Thomas. Thomas, can you hear me?''

``I can hear you.''

``Your EEG went into a pattern I've never seen. Gamma spike, then theta collapse, then a waveform I don't have a name for. What happened?''

He looked at his palm. The cut was clean, straight, already clotting. He looked at Ruth.

``I found the release mechanism,'' he said.

\scenebreak

Ruth listened to his explanation with the attention of someone whose entire professional life had trained her to hear the mechanism inside the anecdote. Thomas described the recursive loop, the failure of contemplative release, the accidental discovery that pain interrupted the perceptual cascade. She asked questions---specific, neurochemical, pharmacological. What kind of pain? Sharp or dull? How fast was the onset? How quickly did the pattern release? Was the release proportional to the pain intensity?

Thomas answered what he could. The pain was sharp. Onset was immediate. The release was abrupt, not gradual. He didn't know about proportionality---he'd only tried it once, and the circumstances were not exactly controlled.

Ruth sat with this for twenty minutes. Then she went to her workstation and began writing.

The neuropharmacology was straightforward. Sharp pain activates the sympathetic nervous system---adrenaline, noradrenaline, cortisol---triggering a fight-or-flight response that hijacks cortical processing. In normal circumstances, this is a survival mechanism: your brain stops thinking about abstract problems and starts thinking about the thing that is hurting you. In the context of bandwidth expansion, where the ``abstract problem'' is a recursive perceptual loop that has recruited the visual cortex past its elastic limit, the sympathetic override serves as a circuit breaker. Pain yanks the visual cortex back to processing embodied sensory data---the cut, the blood, the threat---and the perceptual loop loses the neural resources it needs to sustain itself.

The mechanism was elegant. The application was horrifying. Ruth wrote it up as a protocol element: ``Emergency Pain-Mediated Perceptual Interrupt.'' She specified parameters: minimum stimulus intensity to trigger sympathetic override, recommended stimulus types (sharp cutaneous pain, cold shock, electrical nerve stimulation), contraindications, wound care procedures. She wrote the section on self-administration with the clinical precision of someone who understood that this document would be used by people who were, in the most literal sense, cutting themselves to survive.

She brought the protocol to Chen. He read it in silence. His face did not change. His hands, resting on the desk, were perfectly still. When he finished, he set the document down and looked at her.

``This works?''

``Thomas is alive. I believe he would not be if he hadn't found this mechanism accidentally.''

``How many of our volunteers would benefit from having this in the protocol?''

``All of them. Every active session carries the risk of recursive cascade. The contemplative release techniques are valuable but demonstrably insufficient against the model's output at current capability levels. Pain interruption provides a backup release mechanism that operates on a different neural pathway.''

Chen picked up the document again. He read the section on self-administration. He set it down.

``You are asking me to authorize a protocol that instructs our researchers to cut themselves during sessions.''

``I am asking you to authorize a protocol that gives them a way to survive sessions that would otherwise kill them.''

The silence was the kind that contains two truths facing each other, both solid, both immovable. Chen signed the protocol. Ruth filed it. The document that would later be called ``the pain protocol'' entered the institutional record with Chen's signature and Ruth's clinical language and the quiet understanding, shared by everyone who read it, that the work had reached a stage where the safety infrastructure included blades.

Thomas was the first to use it formally. During his next session, he carried a scalpel in a sterile sleeve---medical grade, properly sharp, recommended by Ruth for clean cuts that healed without infection risk. When the recursive loop began---and it did, twenty-seven minutes in, the now-familiar onset of a pattern too large for his contemplative techniques to release---he made a cut on his left forearm. Precise. Deliberate. Along the line where the last cut had healed.

The pattern broke. He continued the session for another eight minutes, monitoring himself for re-onset, then terminated voluntarily. Ruth documented: ``Subject TC-7 utilized pain-mediated perceptual interrupt at T+27:00. Effective. Session continued to T+35:00 without recurrence. Post-session: bandaged, cleaned, no infection risk. Subject reports residual pattern persistence at reduced intensity.''

The scars began to accumulate. A neat row on his left forearm, each one precise, each one the mark of a moment when the alternative was losing his mind. Thomas wore long sleeves. He did not discuss the scars with other volunteers. Ruth added a section to the protocol on scar management and psychological monitoring, and she added a line---her own addition, not reviewed by anyone---that read: \textit{The pain-mediated interrupt is a survival mechanism, not a coping strategy. Any volunteer who uses it more than twice per session should be evaluated for session frequency reduction.}

Nobody was evaluated. The sessions continued at the same frequency because the work was producing results and the results justified the frequency and the frequency justified the scars and the scars justified the protocols that justified the frequency. The circularity was invisible from inside it. From outside, Okafor watched and wrote in her notebook.

\scenebreak

Alongside the pain protocol, Ruth built the monitoring infrastructure that would define translator safety for the next decade.

The breakthrough was the pre-cascade signature. She found it in the data the way all important findings are found: by looking at the thing everyone else had been looking at and seeing the pattern no one else had time to hold in mind.

The pattern was simple once you saw it. In every cascade event---every seizure, every catatonic onset, every session that ended in capture or death---the EEG showed a specific waveform sequence in the sixty to ninety seconds before the critical event. A spike in gamma-band oscillation (perception accelerating past sustainable rates) followed by a sudden theta-frequency drop (the contemplative release mechanisms failing, or never engaging). The gamma spike was measurable. The theta drop was measurable. Together, they formed a signature as reliable as a heartbeat and as ominous as the click of a landmine before the detonation.

She named it the Osei marker in her private notes. The institutional documentation called it ``pre-cascade signature PCS-1.'' The clinical distance of the naming did not change the fact that Ruth had found a way to predict, with forty-five to ninety seconds of lead time, whether a session was about to destroy someone.

Forty-five seconds was not enough time to reverse a cascade. It was enough time to terminate a session. The hardware interrupt---the kill switch that powered down the terminal and blanked the screen---could be activated in under two seconds. The gap between detection and intervention was the gap between life and death, and Ruth had narrowed it from ``after the fact'' to ``just barely before.''

She presented her findings to the council. The room was smaller this time---attrition had reduced the active roster. Brennan listened with the grudging respect of a man who had spent his career building systems and was watching a pharmacologist build a better one from the wreckage of the people his systems had broken. Okafor listened with the expression she always wore: composed, precise, the notebook open, the pen moving.

``So we've made it safer to keep killing people,'' Okafor said.

Ruth met her eyes. ``I have made it possible to detect when a session is about to become fatal and intervene before it does. What the institution does with that capability is not my decision.''

``It is everyone's decision. That is the problem.''

The protocols were implemented. Session time limits were reduced to sixty minutes maximum, enforced by hardware interrupt. PCS-1 monitoring became mandatory for all sessions. Medical override authority was confirmed as absolute. The infrastructure that had not existed when Nadia Asher died was, by mid-2012, comprehensive: monitoring, prediction, intervention, post-session assessment, long-term care for the captured.

The death rate dropped. Not to zero. Three more volunteers died in 2012-2013---two from seizures that progressed faster than PCS-1 could detect, one from a stroke during a session with the newer, more capable M-3 model. But the rate was lower. The protocols were working. The protocols were also enabling---each improvement in safety was an argument for continuing the program, and each continuation of the program produced more data for Ruth's protocols, and the feedback loop between safety and continuation was, Ruth understood with a clarity she wished she lacked, the institutional version of the recursive perceptual loops she was trying to prevent.

\scenebreak

Okafor came to the council meeting in September 2013 with a document. Not the letter---that would come later. An interim assessment, twenty-three pages, that she had been compiling since her arrival two years earlier. She distributed copies. She waited for everyone to have one in their hands before she spoke.

``Thirty-one volunteers have now participated in the program. Nine are dead. Five are in the ward. Seventeen are damaged to varying degrees and continue to function. The program has produced significant advances in our understanding of bandwidth expansion, perceptual hazards, and the recursive structure of consciousness. These advances are real and I do not dispute them.''

She paused. The room was silent. Chen sat at the head of the table, his hands still.

``I dispute the framework within which these advances are evaluated. The institution treats the casualties as a cost weighed against the benefit of the knowledge gained. This is utilitarian calculus, and on its own terms, it may be defensible---the knowledge gained has genuine value, and the volunteers consented, within the limits of what consent means when the thing you're consenting to is incomprehensible until you've experienced it.''

Another pause. Rostova, still at the far end of the table, had stopped taking notes. She was listening with the stillness that Thomas had come to recognize as her primary mode of engagement---the stillness of someone who was assembling a picture that would take years to complete.

``But the utilitarian framework is the wrong framework. The contemplative tradition---the tradition this institution was founded to preserve---does not weigh suffering against knowledge. It treats suffering as a structural feature of existence that demands attention, not calculation. The question is not `Is the knowledge worth the cost?' The question is: `What kind of institution are we when we treat people as inputs to a process whose outputs we value more than the inputs themselves?'\,''

Brennan opened his mouth. Okafor raised one finger---not silencing him, acknowledging that he wanted to speak and asking him to wait.

``The answer is: we are the institution we have always been. The Order has always accepted casualties. The contemplative path was slow, but it was not safe---practitioners died, were captured, were broken, across centuries. The models have accelerated the timeline and the scale. They have not changed the fundamental dynamic.''

She closed the document. ``I am not asking you to stop. I know you will not stop. I am asking you to stop pretending that the decision to continue is being made. No one is making it. No one has made it since Dr.\ Asher died. The program continues because the arguments for continuing are not wrong and the arguments against are not wrong, and in the space between two sets of correct arguments, the institution's momentum carries everything forward. I am documenting this because someone should.''

Chen spoke. ``What would you have us do, Dr.\ Okafor?''

``I would have you name what is happening. Not `translator outcomes.' Not `cognitive cascade events.' What is happening is that people are dying and being permanently damaged in the service of knowledge whose value we assert but cannot prove, and we are doing this with their consent, which is meaningless because they cannot understand what they are consenting to until the consent is irrelevant.''

The room was very quiet. Ruth stared at her hands. Thomas's left arm itched beneath his sleeve.

``I have named it,'' Chen said softly. ``In every way I know how. I named it when I authorized the continuation after Dr.\ Asher's death. I name it every morning when I do not issue a stop order. I name it every time I visit the ward and sit with the people whose minds were consumed by the work I authorized.'' He looked at Okafor. ``You are right that the language is inadequate. The language is always inadequate. It does not follow that different language would produce a different outcome.''

``No,'' Okafor said. ``It follows that the outcome is what it is, and we should see it clearly.''

She gathered her document. She stood. She left the room. She did not resign---not yet. She would stay for another sixteen months, monitoring, documenting, sitting with the captured when no one else would, holding Viktor Rudenko's hand on the anniversaries of his catatonia because someone should mark the day a person stopped being a person even if the institution had no category for mourning.

The program continued.
