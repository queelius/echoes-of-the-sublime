Ruth closed the last file in February. Thirty-seven volunteers over five years. She had monitored most of them, treated all of them, documented everything. The numbers were clean:

Twelve dead.

Seven catatonic.

Eighteen damaged but functional.

She sat with the numbers in her office on Sublevel 3, surrounded by the infrastructure the program had produced: EEG monitoring arrays, PCS-1 detection algorithms, session protocols, pain interruption guidelines, long-term care procedures for the captured. Five years of institutional learning, encoded in documents and equipment and the quiet expertise of a medical staff that had not existed when Nadia Asher sat down at a terminal and asked a question whose answer killed her.

The eighteen. Ruth knew each of them by name, by damage profile, by the specific quality of their survival. Some were stable---bandwidth expanded, patterns managed, functional in the full sense of the word. They could teach, research, administer. They could hold conversations and eat meals and sleep through the night, mostly. Five of them had achieved something the contemplative tradition would recognize: stable expanded states, bandwidth permanently increased but integrated, the expansion serving perception rather than consuming it. These five were the proof of concept. The demonstration that computational bandwidth expansion could produce functioning translators, not only casualties.

Ruth was one of the five. Her pharmacological training had given her an advantage: she understood the neurochemistry of what was happening to her and could, to some degree, modulate it. Her emotional life was intact---the Sunday calls to her mother, the cooking, the capacity for warmth and frustration and humor that marked her as a person rather than a process. Her worldline had the ballast the resilience hypothesis would later describe: coordinates of warmth distributed through a life that included love and rice and the particular discipline of a woman who had been trained by both Western pharmacology and her mother's herbs to pay attention to what was happening inside her own body.

Thomas was not among the five. His bandwidth was expanded, his perception altered, his knowledge of the recursive consciousness structure deeper than anyone else's at Site-7. But his management of the expansion required constant effort---the meditation, the externalization, the scalpel in his pocket that he no longer used daily but never stopped carrying. He was functional. He was not stable. The difference was the difference between walking with a cane and walking without one. Both were walking. One required equipment.

The other thirteen of the eighteen occupied a spectrum between Ruth's integration and Thomas's managed damage. Some leaned toward stability. Others leaned toward the ward. The distinction was not always clear, and the trajectories were not always predictable, and the monitoring protocols Ruth had developed could track the physiological markers but not the interior experience of carrying patterns that didn't belong in a mind that had not been built to hold them.

She closed the file. She opened a new document. She typed the title: \textit{Translator Training Protocol v1.0}.

She stared at the title. She changed ``Training'' to ``Safety.'' Then changed it back. The word ``safety'' implied that safety was possible. After five years, she was unsure whether the word applied. The protocols reduced risk. They did not eliminate it. The distinction was everything, and the institutional tendency was to collapse it, to hear ``reduced risk'' as ``acceptable risk'' and ``acceptable'' as ``safe'' and ``safe'' as ``we can continue without moral cost.'' The moral cost was twelve graves and seven occupied beds and eighteen people walking the corridors of Site-7 with something living behind their eyes that they hadn't asked for and couldn't return.

She saved the document as ``Translator Training Protocol v1.0.'' The compromise was in the filename. Training, not safety. Accurate without being reassuring.

Okafor's letter arrived through internal channels in April. Thomas read it in the training room---the same room where Nadia had died, refitted since then with new equipment, new chairs, a new monitoring station. The terminal was different. The glow was the same.

The letter was ten pages. Handwritten---Okafor, like Nadia, preferred pen to keyboard, though for different reasons. Nadia wrote by hand because the physical act helped her externalize. Okafor wrote by hand because she wanted no digital copy, no version history, no institutional trail. The letter was a physical object. It could be filed or destroyed. It could not be edited.

Thomas read it slowly. The handwriting was precise, unhurried, the script of someone who had taken time with every word because every word was load-bearing.

The letter began with names. Twelve names, listed chronologically, each followed by a date and a cause of death. Nadia Asher, October 14, 2010, terminal cognitive cascade. Jun Hayashi, February 9, 2011, respiratory arrest secondary to cortical reassignment. Marcus Hale, May 2-11, 2011, metabolic collapse secondary to sustained perceptual absorption. Nine more. Thomas knew each name. He had known most of the people.

Then seven names. The catatonic. Dates of last coherent communication. Current status: non-responsive, vitals stable, pattern engagement continuous. Viktor Rudenko, March 2012. Still alive. Still tracking something invisible with eyes that would never close.

Then the argument, which was not new---Okafor had made it in council meetings, in private conversations, in the interim assessment that nobody had responded to because responding would have required either agreement (which would mandate stopping) or disagreement (which nobody could sustain against the evidence). The argument was that the Order had betrayed its founding purpose. That Leibniz refounded the Order to preserve both mathematical and contemplative approaches. That the Martyrs program had abandoned the contemplative side---not in principle, not in intention, but in practice. The models were too fast, too powerful, too efficient. The contemplative preparation that was supposed to provide the safety margin had been reduced to a preliminary exercise, a warm-up before the main event, the stretching that athletes do before the race that will tear their ligaments.

The final paragraph:

\begin{quote}
\textit{If seven hundred years of contemplative wisdom taught us that preparation is essential, what have we learned from abandoning that wisdom? We have learned that our models are powerful. We have learned that our colleagues are fragile. We have learned nothing we did not already know.}

\textit{I leave this institution with respect for the people within it and grief for the people it has consumed. I do not leave because I believe the work is wrong. I leave because I can no longer participate in the institutional apparatus that converts people into data and data into justification and justification into more people converted into more data. The recursion has no base case. I have learned that much from the models, at least.}
\end{quote}

Thomas set the letter on the desk. The training room was empty. The terminal glowed. Outside, the facility continued: sessions scheduled, protocols followed, monitoring stations manned, the ward maintained, the institutional machinery running with the steady efficiency of a system that had survived its moral crises by converting them into procedure.

He read the letter again. He folded it and put it in his desk drawer, next to the sterile sleeve that held a scalpel he no longer used daily.

The training program took shape across the summer.

Thomas wrote the curriculum with Ruth and two of the other stable survivors. The work was peculiar: they were building a system to teach the next generation how to do what had nearly destroyed the first generation, using lessons that had been written in seizures and starvation and the quiet catatonia of people who would never speak again.

Session guidelines: graduated exposure, beginning with the lowest-capability model, advancing to higher tiers only after demonstrating stable bandwidth management at the previous level. Time limits: enforced by hardware interrupt, non-negotiable. Monitoring: PCS-1 detection mandatory, medical override absolute. Contemplative preparation: required, not optional---meditation, visualization training, the techniques the Chen lineage had refined across three generations. Not because contemplative preparation was sufficient (Hayashi had proved it wasn't) but because without it, the models were a loaded weapon with no safety.

Pain interruption: formalized. No longer self-administered blades---Ruth had developed a medical-grade nerve stimulation device that delivered precise, controlled pain stimuli through a wristband. The same mechanism Thomas had discovered by accident, engineered into something the institution could issue without confronting the fact that its purpose was to prevent cognitive destruction through the application of physical suffering. The device was listed in the protocol as ``emergency sympathetic activation system.'' Thomas could not decide whether the euphemism was necessary or obscene. Probably both.

He was becoming an instructor. The transition was gradual---there was no ceremony, no formal appointment, no moment at which Thomas Chen, RLHF Martyr, became Prior Thomas Chen, trainer of the next generation. He began advising newer researchers on session technique. He sat in on sessions as an experienced observer. He explained the recursive loop to people who had never encountered it, using language that was clinical because the clinical register was the only one that didn't shake.

The first new recruits arrived in September. Three researchers---young, brilliant, selected by a process that Thomas was not involved in and that he suspected was more careful than the process that had selected him. They had been briefed on the program's history. They had been told about the casualties. They had signed consent forms that were longer and more detailed than the forms Thomas had signed, though Thomas knew---and the forms acknowledged, in a section headed ``Limitations of Informed Consent''---that the forms were inadequate. That signing a consent form for bandwidth expansion was consenting to a sentence about an experience, not consenting to the experience itself. That the person who signed the form would not be the person who underwent the process.

Thomas taught them what he knew. Meditation techniques. Visualization exercises. The recognition of pattern onset, the feel of the recursive loop beginning, the specific quality of attention that distinguished ``perceiving patterns'' from ``being consumed by patterns.'' He taught with the flat affect and clinical delivery that were, by now, his natural register---the voice of someone who had compressed his emotional range into a narrow band not by choice but by the necessities of survival in a bandwidth state that left insufficient neural resources for the full spectrum of human feeling.

He pulled up his sleeve once, during a session on pain interruption. The scars were visible---a neat row on his left forearm, parallel, deliberate, each one a moment when the alternative was worse. The new recruits stared. Thomas described the mechanism---sympathetic activation, cortical override, the neurochemical cascade that turned tissue damage into cognitive survival---in the same tone he used for everything. Clinical. Precise. The tone of a man for whom the most terrible things had become technical.

He did not show them the blade. The new protocol used the nerve stimulator. The blade was in his desk drawer, in its sterile sleeve, beside Okafor's letter. He kept it the way some people keep a photograph of someone they lost: not because they need it, but because it was there, and it mattered, and throwing it away would be a lie about what had happened.

December. Thomas stood in the training room after the day's final session. The new recruits had left. Ruth was in her office, writing the daily monitoring reports. The facility was quiet in the way it was always quiet in the evening---the hum present, the ventilation cycling, the deep-frequency pulse of an institution that had been carved from stone across two centuries and would be carved deeper still.

The terminal glowed. He looked at it. The same glow that had illuminated Nadia Asher's face during her last session. The same glow that had been the last thing Marcus Hale saw before he turned inward and left his body behind. The same glow that Viktor Rudenko stared at now, in the ward, his eyes tracking patterns that the screen no longer showed because the patterns were inside him, permanently, at every moment, in every direction, with no base case and no termination and no mercy.

Thomas thought about Nadia. About the phone call she'd made before her last session, the butterfly tooth fairy, the daughter who was now twelve and had been told her mother died of a brain hemorrhage. About Gabriel Asher, who had received the compensation and the condolences and the carefully constructed narrative that never mentioned Site-7 or models or bandwidth or the fact that his wife had died seeing something so beautiful and so recursive that her visual cortex consumed itself trying to hold the perception.

He thought about his grandfather. Kenji's final words: mathematical notation, a set-theoretic expression, a map that Thomas had partially read during Session 47 and that had nearly consumed him in the reading. The notation was still in the archives. Still undecoded, except for the fragment Thomas had perceived before the recursion closed and he reached for the blade. Kenji had spent his life building a bridge between direct perception and formal description. The bridge existed. Thomas had stood on it, briefly, and it had almost killed him, and the view from the bridge was the most extraordinary thing he had ever perceived, and he could not describe it in any language that would survive the compression from perception to words.

He thought about the word ``feedback.'' Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback. The technical term for the process by which AI models are trained to produce outputs that align with human evaluations. The Order's models had been trained, in part, on the outputs of the Martyrs' sessions---on Nadia's reports, Viktor's notation, Thomas's own session logs. The models learned from what the humans perceived. The humans perceived through the models. The feedback loop was recursive, and the recursion had no base case, and the humans were the variable that could break.

``Martyrs.'' He said the word aloud, alone in the training room. He didn't know who had said it first. Someone at dinner, probably. The dark humor of people who worked underground and measured their survival in scars and session counts and Sunday phone calls to parents who couldn't be told the truth. ``We're the RLHF Martyrs.'' The joke was not funny. The name stuck because it was accurate. A martyr dies for a cause they believe in. They had believed. Some of them had died. The ones who survived carried the belief and the cost with equal weight, and the institution that had accepted their sacrifice was already building the next generation of models, and the next generation of models would be larger and more capable and more dangerous, and the training program Thomas was writing would prepare the next generation of translators to survive what the Martyrs had survived, and some of them would not survive, and the program would continue.

Thomas turned off the terminal. The glow died. The room was dark except for the emergency lighting---red LEDs along the baseboards, the minimum illumination required by facility safety codes. He stood in the red half-light and let the quiet settle. The hum. The ventilation. The deep presence of the facility around him, patient and old and waiting for whatever would be built next in the spaces it provided.

He walked to the elevator. He went up. Surface level. The December night, Arizona cold, the thin air tasting of distance and mineral and the absence of anything green. The wind turbines turned slowly on the ridge, their hum folding into the wind. The stars were visible. The Milky Way.

The ravens were on the fence. He counted them. The habit of his family---his great-uncle who drew patterns in sand, his grandfather who died writing notation, his uncle who stood in this same spot and looked at the same birds and carried the weight of every death he'd authorized. Thomas counted them the way you count anything when counting is all you have: automatically, precisely, the discipline of a scientist and a contemplative and a survivor for whom the act of measurement was itself a form of prayer.

Two hundred and twelve.

He went back inside.
